### The Assumption

The license is a means, not an end; sharing knowledge is the goal. Open licenses protect content but not the community's sustainability.

### Operational Reality

The Peeragogy Handbook carries no copyright. CC0 — complete surrender to the public domain. This is not generosity. It is recognition that collaborative work already belongs to everyone who touched it, and claiming ownership over collective intelligence is both impossible and dishonest.

Watch what happens when projects try to maintain control while inviting contribution. Redis changed its license in 2018, pushing commercial users toward paid versions. The community did not just complain — they forked. Valkey emerged with backing from AWS, Google, Oracle. Not because the code was better, but because the license respected what contributors had built together. The original project now competes with its own offspring.

Not ideology. Not principle. Not some abstract commitment to openness. The CC0 choice admits what most collaborative projects refuse to acknowledge — once you invite real participation, you have already lost control. The question is not whether to give up ownership, but whether to do it honestly or pretend you still have it while the work happens anyway.

GitLab shows that pairing license choice with documented governance stabilizes open communities. OpenStreetMap demonstrates that license enforcement requires institutional mechanisms. React's patent clause controversy shows that license terms without community consent create fragility. Terraform's license change shows that license unilateralism triggers structural resistance.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if open licenses are like constitutions — they only work if someone is willing to enforce them, and enforcement is always political?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [GitLab Stewardship Handbook](https://about.gitlab.com/handbook/company/stewardship/) — Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects. Pairing license choice with documented governance stabilizes open communities.
- [OpenStreetMap Data Working Group](https://wiki.osmfoundation.org/wiki/Data_Working_Group) — Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot. License enforcement requires institutional mechanisms.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [React License Controversy (Facebook)](https://code.facebook.com/posts/300798627056246/relicensing-react-patent-commons/) — Corporate-governed open-source can alienate the community through license terms, and community pressure can force governance reversals. License terms without community consent create fragility.
- [Terraform / OpenTofu Fork](https://opentofu.org/) — License changes away from open-source can unite competitors into a fork, demonstrating that ecosystem dependency creates collective bargaining power. License unilateralism triggers structural resistance.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** The license works when paired with explicit governance documentation and formal dispute resolution mechanisms — transparent power boundaries and binding dispute resolution stabilize open communities.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not assume open license solves community sustainability when governance power remains concentrated and opaque — license control without transparent decision-making governance produces forks and alienation.

### Epistemic Synthesis

Formal license becomes self-protection when it corresponds to the real power dynamics of collaborative work. The Redis fork demonstrates that communities hold more leverage than founders assume — but only when the legal framework allows them to exercise it. The useful tension: whether CC0 creates stronger collaborative relationships than protective licenses, or simply makes power struggles more honest.
