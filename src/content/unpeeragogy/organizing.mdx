"Flexible structure." "Fluid roles." "Shared responsibilities." Three phrases that can describe the same reality: no one knows who does what, and the answer is "whoever wants to handle it."

Which translates to: whoever needs it most urgently.

**The urgency default**
The theory presents the organization of learning contexts as an act of collective design. Elegant, democratic, participatory. The practice can be simpler: whoever needs something done most does it. Not because they're the best fit. Because they're the most impatient. Or the most anxious. Or the only one who can't stand seeing the wiki stagnant for three weeks.

This may not be "distributed leadership." It can be "leadership by default." And whoever inherits the work by default gets tired. And when they're tired, the project may find no one ready to take over — because the others never learned to do that work. They had no urgency.

> **Perturbator:** *"'Organic structure' is the most elegant excuse for not writing down who does what. Write it down. On paper. Who updates the wiki, who answers emails, who prepares the agenda. If you don't want to write it, it's because you know the answer is 'always the same person.' And you know that because you are that person."*

**The cost of non-organization**
The theory devotes 260 lines to explaining how to organize co-learning contexts. What it may not discuss is the cost of *not* organizing. Every week where roles are unclear is a week where someone does the work of two people and someone does the work of zero. The first burns out. The second doesn't learn. The project survives by inertia, not health.

And when someone proposes "writing down the roles," they may be accused of bureaucratizing the process. Because "structure must emerge organically." A compelling phrase. In practice, it rarely does.

**What remains**
Organizing may not be a pattern. It may be work. Write down the roles. Assign responsibilities. Accept that someone will have more power than others. If you don't do it explicitly, it will happen implicitly — and the implicit tends to favor whoever has more time, more energy, or more anxiety. Which are rarely the right people for the job.
