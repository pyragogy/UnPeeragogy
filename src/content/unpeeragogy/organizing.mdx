### Reality

The Mozilla community spent years building Firefox through distributed collaboration. Volunteers contributed code, documentation, translations. The project felt genuinely peer-driven — until Mozilla Corporation needed revenue. Google's search deal brought $400 million annually, but also brought Google's priorities into Firefox's roadmap decisions.

Not a betrayal. Not corruption. Not even necessarily wrong.

But it exposed what organizing actually means when someone else owns the infrastructure. The community could debate features, file bugs, submit patches. They could not decide whether to take Google's money or how to spend it. That power lived elsewhere, in a legal entity with different accountability structures than the peer network that built the browser.

Every organizing effort hits this wall eventually. You can coordinate action, build culture, even govern process — but someone owns the servers, holds the trademark, controls the bank account.

> What if organizing isn't about building better coordination tools, but about testing who actually decides when the stakes get real?

### Synthesis

The evidence suggests that sustainable peer learning requires acknowledging power asymmetries rather than designing around them. Projects with explicit governance boundaries — who decides what, when, and with whose money — generate less friction than those that assume pure peer equality.

But this creates its own tension. The more you formalize decision rights, the more you risk reproducing the institutional hierarchies that peer learning was supposed to escape. Open-core companies solve the sustainability problem by accepting that some decisions can't be peer-governed. Community-owned cooperatives maintain peer governance but struggle to scale beyond their founding cohort.

What remains to be tested is whether organizing can hold both realities — the need for clear power structures and the commitment to peer agency — without collapsing into either corporate capture or idealistic paralysis.
