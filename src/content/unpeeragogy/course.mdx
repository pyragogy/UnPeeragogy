### Il Dogma

Courses are structured learning experiences that can fail when they lack clear organization or when expectations are misaligned with the peer learning model. The theory assumes that better course design — clearer objectives, better facilitation, tighter feedback loops — produces better peer learning outcomes.

### La Realtà

Fabrizio watches another cohort stumble through his maker workshop. Day three: half the participants still ask when he'll "teach the real stuff." They want lectures. They want steps. They want him to stand at the front and deliver knowledge like packages through a slot.

He doesn't.

The course structure exists — materials, timelines, peer feedback loops. But the learning happens in the gaps between what he provides and what they need to figure out. Not because he's withholding information. Not because discovery learning is pedagogically superior. Not because he believes in some romantic vision of self-directed growth. Because the moment he starts teaching, they stop learning how to learn from each other.

The institutional partnerships keep the lights on. The defined facilitator roles give nervous administrators something to put in their reports. But the real work — the peer-to-peer transmission of tacit knowledge — happens despite the course structure, not because of it.

### Perturbatore

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if every course designer thinks they've found the sweet spot between structure and freedom — but most have just found the sweet spot between two different kinds of failure?"*

### Evidenze di Tenuta

**p2pu.org** — Peer learning models with defined facilitator roles and institutional partnerships are more sustainable than purely peer-driven learning. P2PU shows that structured facilitation with institutional backing creates conditions where peer learning can survive.

**wiki.osmfoundation.org** — Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot. OpenStreetMap demonstrates that structured authority mechanisms — not course design — resolve the conflicts that kill peer learning.

### Evidenze di Faglia

**w3.org** — Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes. WebAssembly shows that the sweet spot between structure and freedom is always a trade-off, never a solution.

**ietf.org** — Rough consensus governance only works when backed by hierarchical escalation paths. The IETF demonstrates that pure peer governance without escalation fails — not because of poor course design, but because consensus without appeals is governance by attrition.

### Euristiche Operative

**Regola Positiva:** Applica questo pattern quando il peer learning è combinato con ruoli di facilitazione definiti e supporto istituzionale. P2PU dimostra che la facilitazione strutturata con partnership istituzionali crea le condizioni per la sopravvivenza del peer learning.

**Regola Negativa:** Non applicare governance peer pura quando i corsi coinvolgono decisioni tecniche contestate o allocazione di risorse. IETF e WebAssembly dimostrano che il consenso senza percorsi di escalation fallisce — e il design del corso non risolve questa tensione strutturale.

**Stato:** PROVVISORIO

L'evidenza suggerisce qualcosa di scomodo: le strutture formali che costruiamo — obiettivi di apprendimento, protocolli di facilitazione, rubriche di valutazione — servono i bisogni istituzionali più dell'apprendimento. Il corso crea le condizioni per il peer learning, ma non crea l'apprendimento stesso. Troppa struttura e le persone delegano al sistema. Troppa poca e annegano. Il punto non è un equilibrio — è un'instabilità produttiva dove gli studenti non possono stabilizzarsi nel consumo passivo.
