### The Assumption

Courses are structured learning experiences that can fail when they lack clear organization or when expectations are misaligned with the peer learning model. Better course design — clearer objectives, better facilitation, tighter feedback loops — produces better peer learning outcomes.

### Operational Reality

The workshop materials exist — timelines, peer feedback loops, structured activities. But the learning happens in the gaps between what the facilitator provides and what participants need to figure out. Not because the facilitator is withholding information. Not because discovery learning is pedagogically superior. Not because of some romantic vision of self-directed growth. Because the moment the facilitator starts teaching, participants stop learning how to learn from each other.

Courses in peer learning serve a paradoxical function: the formal structures — learning objectives, facilitation protocols, assessment rubrics — serve institutional needs more than learning. The course creates the conditions for peer learning, but does not create the learning itself. Too much structure and people delegate to the system. Too little and they drown. The point is not an equilibrium — it is a productive instability where participants cannot stabilize in passive consumption.

P2PU demonstrates that structured facilitation with institutional backing creates conditions where peer learning can survive. OpenStreetMap shows that structured authority mechanisms — not course design — resolve the conflicts that kill peer learning. WebAssembly reveals that the sweet spot between structure and freedom is always a trade-off, never a solution. The IETF demonstrates that pure peer governance without escalation fails — not because of poor course design, but because consensus without appeals is governance by attrition.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if every course designer thinks they have found the sweet spot between structure and freedom — but most have just found the sweet spot between two different kinds of failure?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [P2PU Learning Circles](https://www.p2pu.org/en/learning-circles/) — Peer learning models with defined facilitator roles and institutional partnerships are more sustainable than purely peer-driven learning. Structured facilitation with institutional backing creates conditions where peer learning can survive.
- [OpenStreetMap Data Working Group](https://wiki.osmfoundation.org/wiki/Data_Working_Group) — Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot. Structured authority mechanisms — not course design — resolve the conflicts that kill peer learning.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [WebAssembly Community Group](https://www.w3.org/community/webassembly/) — Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes. The sweet spot between structure and freedom is always a trade-off, never a solution.
- [IETF Working Group Rough Consensus](https://www.ietf.org/about/) — Rough consensus governance only works when backed by hierarchical escalation paths. Pure peer governance without escalation fails — consensus without appeals is governance by attrition.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Design courses as productive instability systems where the container is explicit but the learning path is not — P2PU demonstrates that institutional backing and defined facilitator roles create the conditions for peer learning without replacing it.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not design courses assuming pure peer governance suffices when they involve contested technical decisions or resource allocation — IETF and WebAssembly demonstrate that consensus without escalation paths fails; course design does not resolve this structural tension.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The evidence suggests something uncomfortable: the formal structures we build — learning objectives, facilitation protocols, assessment rubrics — serve institutional needs more than learning. The course creates the conditions for peer learning, but does not create the learning itself. Too much structure and people delegate to the system. Too little and they drown. The point is not equilibrium — it is a productive instability where participants cannot stabilize in passive consumption.
