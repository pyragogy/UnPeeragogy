### Claim

> goals goes further than "learning" or "working" in their mainstream
> definitions. This article will look at examples drawn from Linux,

### Reality

The coworking space on Via Tortona fills with MacBooks at 9 AM. Designers nod at developers. Consultants exchange cards. Everyone pays €25 for a day pass to sit near other people doing unrelated work. This isn't collaboration — it's parallel isolation with better coffee.

Real peer production happens differently. Linux kernel development runs on brutal meritocracy and clear patch acceptance rules. Wikipedia editors fight over citations and sourcing standards. PlanetMath contributors argue about mathematical rigor. Not pleasant. Not smooth. But these systems produce lasting work because they've built governance structures that survive conflict. Coworking spaces offer proximity without process, presence without purpose.

The friction matters more than the coffee.

> What if the real barrier to collaboration isn't distance but the absence of useful disagreement?

### Evidence That Supports

**kernel.org** -- Clear rules for patch acceptance and defined backporting processes produce reliable long-term maintenance in peer production.

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that sustainable peer production requires more than shared space — it demands shared standards for what counts as contribution. Linux succeeds not because programmers like each other, but because they've agreed on how to evaluate patches. Wikipedia endures not through consensus, but through documented processes for handling editorial disputes.

Coworking may actually inhibit collaboration by creating the illusion that proximity equals partnership. When governance structures remain informal, communities default to the path of least resistance: polite coexistence rather than productive conflict. What remains to be tested is whether spaces designed for friction rather than comfort might generate different outcomes — places where the work of building shared standards becomes visible, not hidden behind the performance of getting along.
