### The Assumption

Coworking is a foundational mode of peer production, often confused with collaboration despite lacking shared projects or goals. Coworking can lead to collaboration but the theory warns against assuming automatic progression from proximity to partnership.

### Operational Reality

The coworking space fills with MacBooks at 9 AM. Designers nod at developers. Consultants exchange cards. Everyone pays for a day pass to sit near other people doing unrelated work. This is not collaboration — it is parallel isolation with better coffee.

Real peer production happens differently. Linux kernel development runs on brutal meritocracy and clear patch acceptance rules. Wikipedia editors fight over citations and sourcing standards. PlanetMath contributors argue about mathematical rigor. Not pleasant. Not smooth. But these systems produce lasting work because they have built governance structures that survive conflict. Coworking spaces offer proximity without process, presence without purpose.

Linux demonstrates that coordination at scale requires explicit processes, not shared physical space. LWN shows that structured communication channels — not coworking proximity — sustain distributed coordination. Wikipedia demonstrates that even in communities with shared purpose, communication requires governance. Drupal shows that moving from informal to formal coordination requires structural redesign, not just shared space.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if coworking without governance is just expensive coffee and the illusion of productivity — and the real barrier to collaboration is not distance but the absence of useful disagreement?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [Linux Kernel Stable Tree Governance](https://www.kernel.org/doc/html/latest/process/stable-kernel-rules.html) — Clear rules for patch acceptance and defined backporting processes produce reliable long-term maintenance in peer production. Coordination at scale requires explicit processes, not shared physical space.
- [LWN Kernel Development Summaries](https://lwn.net/Kernel/) — Independent third-party summaries of peer production activity create shared awareness that enables distributed governance at scale. Structured communication channels — not coworking proximity — sustain distributed coordination.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [Wikipedia Signpost Community Journalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Wikipedia_Signpost) — Community-produced summaries become de facto governance communication channels that can diverge from official narratives. Even communities with shared purpose require governance of their communication.
- [Drupal Governance](https://www.drupal.org/governance) — Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model required years of structural work, not organic emergence. Moving from informal to formal coordination requires structural redesign, not just shared space.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Coworking works at scale when supported by clear coordination rules and independent communication channels — Linux and LWN demonstrate that explicit contribution acceptance processes and structured communication channels sustain distributed coordination.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not assume coworking naturally transforms into collaboration without explicit governance — Drupal demonstrates that moving from informal to formal coordination requires structural redesign, not just shared space.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The evidence suggests that sustainable peer production requires more than shared space — it requires shared standards for what counts as a contribution. Linux does not work because programmers like each other, but because they have agreed on how to evaluate patches. Coworking can inhibit collaboration by creating the illusion that proximity equals partnership. The friction matters more than the coffee.
