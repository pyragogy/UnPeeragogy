### Claim

The Peeragogy Project demonstrates how peer learning initiatives can produce collaborative outputs like handbooks while maintaining ongoing community engagement through regular meetings and sustained participation.

### Reality

A project dies when it becomes a monument to its own process. The Peeragogy Handbook speaks of resources and sustainability — careful words that dodge the central question of what happens when peer production meets its own bureaucracy. Documentation multiplies. Meetings proliferate. The work that drew people in gets buried under the work of managing the work.

Not consensus. Not meritocracy. Not goodwill distributed through digital channels. These are the artifacts of projects that mistake process for purpose. Real peer production happens in the friction between what people want to build and what the container allows them to build. The moment you can predict how a decision will be made, you've stopped making decisions that matter.

The useful friction lives in the boundaries that resist — not the ones that organize.

> What if the project container is designed to break before the work inside it fossilizes?

### Synthesis

The evidence suggests something harder than governance theory admits. Explicit boundaries work when the work stays within them. Formal rules sustain production when production follows predictable patterns. But peer projects that matter tend to exceed their containers — the interesting work happens at the edges where the process hasn't caught up yet.

What remains untested is whether a project can build its own resistance to institutionalization. Whether the container can stay porous enough to let the work breathe without losing the structure that makes collaboration possible. The tension between sustainability and vitality — one demands predictability, the other feeds on surprise.
