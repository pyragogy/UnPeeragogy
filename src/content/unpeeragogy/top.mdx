### Claim

The Peeragogy Handbook presents a top ten list of patterns as the most important and accessible entry points for understanding peer learning, positioning these patterns as definitive guides for practitioners.

### Reality

The Node.js community split in 2015. Not over code. Not over features. Over who gets to decide what goes into the next release. The Foundation model promised shared governance, but when push came to shove, corporate sponsors still held the real power — the trademark, the infrastructure, the release keys. The community forked to io.js because they couldn't get straight answers about who actually controlled what.

Wikipedia's Arbitration Committee faces the same friction from a different angle. Volunteer editors spend years building expertise, then find themselves overruled by a committee structure that mirrors corporate hierarchy more than peer collaboration. The people doing the work don't control the final decisions. ArbCom members gain authority not through demonstrated editing skill but through community politics — a different kind of top-down, dressed in democratic language.

Both cases expose the same gap. The rhetoric says "community-driven" but the structure says "management by committee." Real power concentrates at designated control points while the actual peer-to-peer work happens elsewhere.

> What if every hierarchy we build to "serve the community" actually serves to distance us from each other?

### Synthesis

These governance failures suggest something uncomfortable about how we organize peer learning. The moment we formalize leadership, we create a new kind of distance — not just between leaders and participants, but between the work itself and the people doing it. The Node.js split wasn't really about technical disagreements. It was about trust breaking down when power became opaque.

What remains to be tested is whether genuine peer governance can scale without recreating the same patterns it claims to replace. The Wikipedia case shows how even well-intentioned democratic structures can drift toward the very hierarchies they were designed to avoid — committees that make decisions about work they don't do, for communities they represent but don't fully participate in.
