The handbook says this section is "currently rather sparse, since most of the case studies have been moved elsewhere." The most honest sentence in the manual, arguably.

**What cooperation can look like**
Cooperation, in theory, is working together. Harmony. Synergy. In practice, cooperation can mean: people with different agendas trying not to undermine each other while working on the same project. Conflict is not an exception to cooperation — it may be the raw material of cooperation. Without conflict, what emerges may be passive alignment, not genuine cooperation.

But the theory tends to focus on co-facilitation, workscape, participation. All words that describe the surface without necessarily touching the tension underneath. Because talking about conflict means admitting that patterns aren't enough — that political, emotional, relational labor is needed, which no pattern can fully codify.

> **Perturbator:** *"Pretending cooperation means 'getting along' misses the point. Cooperation means managing the fact that you don't agree and moving forward anyway. If you're not arguing, you may not be cooperating — you may be postponing the explosion. And the explosion always comes — usually after the project deadline."*

**The case of the empty section**
It's interesting that the Cooperation section is "rather sparse." Why? Because the case studies were moved elsewhere. But perhaps — and this is a provocation worth considering — the section is sparse because genuine cooperation is rare. Most collaborative projects are not authentic cooperation. They are mutual tolerance under deadline. And tolerance isn't cooperation. It's just not-killing-each-other. Which is different.

**What remains**
Cooperation doesn't always emerge from patterns. It can emerge from the group's ability to sustain conflict. If you can't argue well, you may not be able to cooperate well. Patterns can help you argue better — but they can't replace the work of learning how to do it. And that work may not be learnable from a manual.
