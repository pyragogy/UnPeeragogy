### Claim

The Peeragogy Handbook treats cooperation as a central component of peer learning, supported by shared goals, participation, and collective knowledge building. The model assumes that these dynamics can sustain collaboration between participants who share enough common ground to work together productively. Cooperation, in the theory, is the natural expression of a well-functioning peer group.

### Reality

Cooperation becomes visible when disagreement surfaces. Not because the group was not cooperating before — but because harmony hides the mechanisms that hold the group together.

When everyone agrees, you cannot tell if the group is cooperating or just mirroring each other. When disagreement emerges, you see what the group is made of: does it find a way to continue acting together, or does it fragment?

Peeragogy describes cooperation as a condition you build toward. Practice treats it as a capacity that reveals itself under pressure.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if we are confusing cooperation with harmony? A group can agree on everything and produce nothing together. It can also keep working while its members strongly disagree. Maybe the point is not the absence of conflict — but the capacity to move through it without losing the ability to act."*

### Evidence That Supports

**GitLab stewardship handbook** — GitLab Inc and the open-core community signed a written pact defining where corporate strategy ends and community autonomy begins. The boundaries were explicit before conflict arrived. When tension came, the structure channeled it without destroying the group's ability to produce.

**Mondragon financial crisis** — A cooperatives with multi-layer governance (councils, internal banks, containment mechanisms). When the crisis hit, the structure contained the conflict. The group survived not because people were exceptionally cooperative — but because governance had been built before it was needed.

### Evidence That Complicates

**Wikipedia Arbitration Committee** — Informal cooperation failed systematically in certain types of editorial disputes. Wikipedia created a formal judicial body to handle what peer consensus could not. This could mean informal cooperation is insufficient for high-stakes conflict. It could also mean that Wikipedia's scale requires mechanisms that small groups do not need.

**WebAssembly Community Group** — Light governance was chosen intentionally to keep development velocity high. Predictable failure modes emerged: contested decisions, asymmetric participation, occasional paralysis. Cooperation without governance can look like cooperation — until a decision needs to be made and nobody has the authority to make it.

### Evidence That Contradicts

*No case in the current corpus directly contradicts the pattern.*

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**OpenStreetMap Data Working Group** — Formal dispute resolution with binding authority resolves conflicts when informal consensus fails. But the group is small, long-tenured, and self-selected. Whether the structure would scale or generalize is not known.

### Synthesis

What these cases suggest: building the container before the conflict arrives may increase a group's capacity to move through disagreement without fragmenting.

GitLab and Mondragon had governance structures ready before they were tested. Wikipedia and WebAssembly built theirs reactively — after the failure was already visible. The difference is not clearly about the quality of the people involved. It may be about whether the structure exists when you need it.

Perhaps, in some contexts, trust does not precede structure. Trust can be produced by the predictability of the structure itself. This is an interesting hypothesis. It needs more cases to test.
