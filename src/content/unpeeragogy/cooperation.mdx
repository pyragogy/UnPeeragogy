### Claim

Peeragogy says cooperation is what happens when a group works together harmoniously. The theory presents it as a natural outcome of shared goals, mutual respect, and open communication. Get the right people in a room with the right intentions, and cooperation emerges.

### Reality

Cooperation happens because you disagree. If you agreed, you wouldn't need it. Two people who agree on everything don't cooperate — they mirror each other. Cooperation is what happens *after* the disagreement surfaces and the group does not collapse.

Theory describes cooperation as harmony. Practice knows it's logistics. You don't cooperate because you like each other. You cooperate because you need something done that you cannot do alone, and the person next to you is the one who can help — despite being wrong about half the things you care about.

> **Perturbatore:** *"You think cooperating means getting along. It doesn't. Cooperating means delivering results despite not getting along. The group that smiles through every disagreement isn't cooperating. It's deferring. Real cooperation starts when someone says 'I disagree' and the group doesn't fall apart. That's the first cooperative act. Everything before that is just being polite."*

### Positive Examples

**GitLab stewardship handbook** — GitLab Inc and the open-core community. A written pact that says: you do yours, we do ours, here's where your money ends and your autonomy begins. It worked because the boundaries were explicit before the conflict arrived.

**Mondragon financial crisis** — Multi-layer governance structure with councils, internal banks, containment mechanisms. When the crisis hit, the structure channeled conflict without destroying the group. It did not happen by accident. It happened because governance existed before it was needed.

### Negative Examples

**Wikipedia Arbitration Committee** — Informal cooperation failed systematically in editorial disputes. Wikipedia had to create a formal judicial body — the Arbitration Committee — because conflict does not catalyze cooperation. It escalates. A third authority was required.

**WebAssembly Community Group** — Light governance was chosen to keep development fast. Predictable failure modes followed: contested decisions, asymmetric participation, decision paralysis. Cooperation without governance is not cooperation. It is postponement.

### Synthesis

What works in the real world: build the container before the conflict arrives.

GitLab and Mondragon both had governance structures ready *before* they were tested. Wikipedia and WebAssembly built theirs reactively — after the failure was already visible. The difference is not the quality of the people. The difference is whether the structure exists when you need it.

If you want cooperation in a peer group, do not invest in team-building. Invest in boundaries: who decides what, where money ends and autonomy begins, what happens when someone disagrees. The trust will follow the structure, not the other way around.
