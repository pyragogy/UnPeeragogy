### Reality

## REALITY

A maker spaces opens its doors wide. Free workshops, shared tools, community boards covered in project photos. The founders beam about "radical sharing" and "democratizing making." Six months later, the space runs on three volunteers who know where everything is kept, while newcomers wander confused past broken 3D printers nobody maintains.

Not the tools. Not the space. Not the enthusiasm.

The human part — who fixes what breaks, who teaches the next person, who decides when someone's hogging the laser cutter for a month. Sharing creates access. It doesn't create the invisible work that makes access meaningful.

I've watched this pattern kill more peer learning projects than bad funding ever did. The moment you say "everyone owns this," you discover that nobody does.

> What if the problem isn't getting people to share, but getting them to take responsibility for what they've shared?

### Synthesis

## SYNTHESIS

These cases suggest something uncomfortable about peer governance. The spaces that survive aren't the ones that share most freely — they're the ones that make the maintenance work visible and distribute it intentionally. A makerspace with clear tool stewards outlasts the one with "community ownership." The learning circle with rotating facilitation beats the one where "we're all equals here."

What remains to be tested is whether this applies beyond physical spaces. Digital commons face the same tension — between the ideal of open contribution and the reality that someone has to merge the pull requests, moderate the forums, keep the servers running.

The distinction that emerges isn't between sharing and hoarding, but between sharing with structure and sharing into the void.
