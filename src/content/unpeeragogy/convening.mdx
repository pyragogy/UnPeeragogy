### The Assumption

The Peeragogy Handbook states that convening is easy but often leads to unproductive meetings without a clear plan, and that groups often die between invitation and first meeting due to lack of specific proposals. Fix the planning, fix the convening.

### Operational Reality

Fabrizio calls a meeting for Tuesday. By Monday, three people have scheduling conflicts, two have not responded, and one suggests moving to Slack instead. The original agenda — ambitious, necessary — dissolves into logistics. This is convening in practice: the gap between intention and attendance, between the group you imagine and the group that shows up.

Not coordination failure. Not poor planning. Not the wrong tools. The friction lives deeper — in the assumption that gathering creates alignment, that proximity produces purpose. The handbook talks about "suitable groups" as if suitability were obvious, as if the right people would recognize each other and naturally cohere. But convening exposes what remains hidden in theory — that someone must decide who belongs, someone must hold the center when others drift away.

CPAN demonstrates that convening with clear ownership rules from day one prevents the invitation-to-collapse pipeline. P2PU shows that convening works when someone is responsible for holding the container — not just issuing invitations. Drupal reveals that convening without planning the authority transition creates crises that no agenda can fix. cMOOCs show that pure peer models do not scale beyond the intimacy threshold.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if you cannot convene your way out of the fact that someone has to make the hard calls — and the people who made them before the meeting started are the ones who hold the power when it ends?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [CPAN/PAUSE Module Governance](https://www.cpan.org/modules/PAUSE/) — Simple namespace ownership with easy transfer and clear permissions can scale decentralized peer production with minimal governance overhead. Convening with clear ownership rules from day one prevents the invitation-to-collapse pipeline.
- [P2PU Learning Circles](https://www.p2pu.org/en/learning-circles/) — Peer learning models with defined facilitator roles and institutional partnerships are more sustainable than purely peer-driven learning. Convening works when someone is responsible for holding the container — not just issuing invitations.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [Drupal Governance](https://www.drupal.org/governance) — Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model required years of structural work, not organic emergence. Convening without planning the authority transition creates crises that no agenda can fix.
- [cMOOC vs xMOOC Pedagogical Governance](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/259999056_Connectivist_MOOCs_and_xMOOCs) — The divide in open education between peer-governed and institution-governed models shows that peer governance has known scalability limits. Pure peer models do not scale beyond the intimacy threshold.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Apply this pattern when the group remains small or adopts hybrid governance from the start — CPAN (namespace ownership) and P2PU (institutional partnerships) demonstrate that defined authority mechanisms make convening sustainable.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not rely on pure peer convening when the group will exceed 15 active members or manage contested resources without explicit authority structures — Drupal and cMOOCs demonstrate that convening without planning authority transitions creates crises that no agenda can resolve.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The theoretical pattern assumes convening is a logistical problem — a clear agenda and a date suffice. Evidence shows that convening creates power: someone decides who belongs, someone holds the center when others drift away. Convening is not the beginning of peer learning. It is the moment when authority constitutes itself — and not admitting this is the first failure.
