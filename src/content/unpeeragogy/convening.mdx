## Summary

Convening creates the illusion of shared decision-making while avoiding the question of who decides when peers disagree. Theory promises emergent governance — practice demands someone willing to make the unpopular calls. Most peer learning projects fail not from lack of participation but from the refusal to name authority.

## Euristiche Operative

### Positive Rule

> This pattern works when groups remain small or adopt hybrid governance from inception. CPAN's simple namespace ownership and P2PU's institutional partnerships show that defined authority mechanisms enable sustainable peer production at scale.

### Negative Rule

> Do not apply this pattern when the group will exceed 15 active members or handle contested resources without explicit authority structures. Drupal's governance transition and cMOOC scalability limits show that peer governance requires years of structural work that convening alone cannot provide.

## Sintesi Obliqua

The pattern assumes convening solves the governance problem by creating buy-in through participation. Partial verification — it works when groups stay under 15 members or adopt explicit authority from day one. CPAN's namespace ownership and P2PU's institutional partnerships show that defined power structures enable peer production, while Drupal's governance transition and cMOOC scaling failures reveal that peer governance requires years of structural work that convening cannot provide. The real mechanism isn't participation design but authority allocation — who gets to say no, and when. Convening is just postponing the decision about who decides.
