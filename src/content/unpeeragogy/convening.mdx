### Claim

The Peeragogy Handbook states that convening is easy but often leads to unproductive meetings without a clear plan, and that groups often die between invitation and first meeting due to lack of specific proposals.

### Reality

Fabrizio calls a meeting for Tuesday. By Monday, three people have scheduling conflicts, two haven't responded, and one suggests moving to Slack instead. The original agenda — ambitious, necessary — dissolves into logistics. This is convening in practice: the gap between intention and attendance, between the group you imagine and the group that shows up.

Not coordination failure. Not poor planning. Not the wrong tools. The friction lives deeper — in the assumption that gathering creates alignment, that proximity produces purpose. Every convener learns this the hard way: the people who need to be there most are often the ones who can't make it work.

The handbook talks about "suitable groups" as if suitability were obvious, as if the right people would recognize each other and naturally cohere. But convening exposes what remains hidden in theory — that someone must decide who belongs, someone must hold the center when others drift away.

> > What if the risk isn't that your convening will fail, but that it will succeed in ways you never intended?"*

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that convening carries a deeper risk than poor attendance or weak engagement. The act of gathering creates power — someone decides the agenda, someone manages the boundaries, someone determines what counts as progress. The peer learning literature celebrates distributed authority, but convening reveals the persistent need for someone to make the hard calls about direction and membership.

What remains to be tested is whether this tension can be made productive rather than resolved. The most honest conveners may be those who acknowledge the authority they're exercising — not to apologize for it, but to make it visible and accountable to the group they're trying to serve.
