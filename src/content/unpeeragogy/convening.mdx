The theory dedicates 209 lines to how to convene a group. The invitation, creating a safe space, listening to motivations. What it may not say: convening people is the easy part. Keeping them together is the hard part.

**When the first meeting is also the last full one**
Convening is sending an invitation. Easy. Keeping them together after the third meeting is a different question. The theory can present convening as an act of heroic organization. In practice, the first meeting is often the last time everyone is present. After that, attrition begins. One person misses a meeting, then another, then the group meets with half the participants and no one mentions it.

> **Perturbator:** *"The first meeting is always full. The fourth is always empty. That's not a convening problem. It's a retention problem. You invited everyone. You didn't give anyone a reason to stay after the first week. Convening is never enough."*

**The paradox of inclusion**
The theory advises: "create a safe space, listen to everyone, be inclusive." In some contexts, the result can be: no one decides, everyone talks, the group grows but doesn't produce. Inclusion becomes an end, not a means. The group meets to meet, not to do.

And when someone proposes closing the group or setting a participant limit, they may be accused of elitism. The question worth asking: does a group of 20 produce less than a group of 5? And is the trade-off between reach and throughput worth examining?

**What remains**
Convening may be the simplest gesture of collaboration. It may not need 209 lines to explain. It might need two: "Invite those who want to participate. Then pay attention to who actually participates. Everyone else is audience." Everything else is commentary.
