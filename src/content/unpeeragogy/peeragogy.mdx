### Claim

This pattern is relevant to anyone who wants to do active learning

### Reality

Wikipedia's administrators don't call themselves administrators. They call themselves "janitors" — people who clean up after the real work happens. This linguistic dodge reveals something useful about how peer production actually functions. The hierarchy exists, but it refuses to name itself as such.

I've watched three peer learning communities collapse in the past five years. Not from lack of enthusiasm or good intentions. From the credentialing crisis. Someone always asks: "How do we know this person actually knows what they're teaching?" The question kills the peer fiction instantly. Either you build quality gates — which means hierarchy — or you accept that some of what gets shared is wrong, incomplete, or actively harmful.

The Wikipedia model works because it separates content creation from content validation. Peers write. Algorithms and editors police. But most peer learning projects try to make the same people do both jobs. The friction becomes unbearable.

> > What if peer governance isn't about eliminating hierarchy, but about making it honest — visible, temporary, and revocable?"*

### Evidence That Supports

**apache.org** -- Structured consensus with formal voting rules and meritocratic advancement can sustain peer production for decades.

**about.gitlab.com** -- Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.


### Evidence That Complicates

**p2pu.org** -- Peer learning communities inevitably face credentialing and quality assurance crises that require either hierarchy or external validation frameworks.

**researchgate.net** -- The divide in open education between peer-governed and institution-governed models shows that peer governance has known scalability limits.

### Synthesis

The cases suggest that sustainable peer production requires what we might call "useful friction" — intentional constraints that prevent the system from optimizing itself into either chaos or rigid hierarchy. Wikipedia's endless edit wars aren't a bug. They're the price of keeping knowledge open and contested.

What remains to be tested is whether peer learning can develop similar mechanisms. The current models either collapse under quality pressure or gradually bureaucratize into the very institutions they sought to replace. The distinction may not be between hierarchical and peer governance, but between governance that admits its limits and governance that pretends they don't exist.
