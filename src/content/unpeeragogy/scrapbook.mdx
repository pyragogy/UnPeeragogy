### Claim

This pattern describes a way to make the project meaningful.

### Reality

The scrapbook sits in every team's shared drive. Notes from brainstorming sessions. Screenshots of competitor features. Links to articles someone thought were relevant. The folder grows — 47 items, 112 items, 203 items. Nobody deletes anything because it might be useful later.

I watched a startup spend six months building their "innovation pipeline" around a scrapbook mentality. Every interesting idea went into the shared Notion database. Every market insight got tagged and filed. The CEO called it their "strategic memory." The team called it something else when leadership wasn't listening. Not a resource. Not a reference. A graveyard with good lighting.

The pattern promises meaning through accumulation. Collect enough fragments and sense will emerge. But meaning isn't additive — it's selective. The scrapbook becomes a monument to everything the team couldn't decide to abandon.

> What if the scrapbook's real function isn't to preserve ideas but to quarantine them?

### Synthesis

The cases suggest that scrapbooks serve a different purpose than advertised. They don't organize knowledge — they organize guilt. Every unfinished thought gets a dignified burial instead of honest deletion. The team maintains the fiction that scattered fragments constitute progress.

What remains to be tested is whether intentional curation creates more friction than passive accumulation. The explicit governance boundaries that reduce friction in open-core projects may apply here — clear rules about what enters the scrapbook and what gets expelled. But this requires admitting that most captured ideas deserve to die, not just hibernate.

The useful friction isn't in collecting everything. It's in the repeated choice of what not to keep — the honest work of letting good ideas go when better ones demand the space.
