### Claim

For [Ward Cunningham](http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ward_cunningham)

### Reality

The Peeragogy Handbook calls wikis "useful and powerful collaboration tools" for peer learning. I've watched three wiki projects die in the last two years. Not from technical failure — from human exhaustion. The MediaWiki software ran fine. The servers stayed up. People just stopped caring enough to fight the endless revert wars over stub articles and formatting disputes.

Wikipedia works because it has something most learning wikis lack — a massive population willing to police each other's contributions. Your peer learning group of twelve people doesn't have that luxury. When Sarah deletes Tom's carefully researched section because "it doesn't fit the structure," and Tom responds by removing Sarah's entire page, you need someone with actual authority to make a decision. Not consensus. Not discussion. Authority.

The wiki-as-garden metaphor breaks down when you realize gardens need gardeners — not committees of gardeners arguing about whether roses belong next to tomatoes.

> What if the problem isn't that wikis are too open, but that we pretend openness scales without acknowledging the governance work it demands?

### Evidence That Supports

**about.gitlab.com** -- Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.

**wiki.osmfoundation.org** -- Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot.


### Evidence That Complicates

**w3.org** -- Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes for peer participation.


### Evidence That Contradicts

*No case in the current corpus directly contradicts the pattern.*

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**ietf.org** -- Rough consensus governance only works when backed by hierarchical escalation paths; pure consensus without appeals fails.


### Synthesis

These cases suggest that wiki success depends less on the technology than on the willingness to establish and enforce editorial boundaries. The projects that survived had clear hierarchies — someone who could say "this stays, this goes" without requiring group consensus on every edit. The ones that died treated equal access as equal authority.

What remains to be tested is whether peer learning groups can explicitly negotiate these power structures before launching collaborative writing projects. The useful friction may not be in the editing process itself — but in the honest conversation about who gets to make final decisions when peers disagree.
