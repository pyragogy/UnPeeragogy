### Claim

The Peeragogy Handbook emphasizes that patterns must be practiced, not just read. It positions practice as the bridge between theoretical knowledge and real-world application, warning that without practice, peeragogical groups risk becoming discussion clubs rather than action-oriented communities.

### Reality

The Peeragogy Handbook dedicates 200 pages to patterns of peer learning. Governance structures, feedback loops, collaborative protocols — all mapped with academic precision. Meanwhile, three months into our reading group, we had four people showing up to discuss chapters nobody had finished reading.

Not broken process. Not wrong people. Not bad timing.

The gap between documented practice and lived practice.

I've watched this pattern repeat across maker spaces, open source projects, community gardens. The documentation grows more sophisticated while participation shrinks. We mistake the map for walking.

> > What if the real practice isn't following the patterns, but discovering why they don't work for you?"*

### Evidence That Supports

**about.gitlab.com** -- Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.


### Evidence That Complicates

**drupal.org** -- Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model requires years of structural work, not organic emergence.


### Evidence That Contradicts

*No case in the current corpus directly contradicts the pattern.*

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**en.wikipedia.org** -- Wikipedia's consensus processes produce de facto hierarchies through participation inequality — those who show up consistently gain disproportionate influence.


### Synthesis

The Peeragogy patterns suggest something useful about learning design, but they may function better as diagnostic tools than prescriptive frameworks. When a reading group fails, the handbook's governance patterns reveal what we assumed but never negotiated — who decides what we read, how we handle absent members, what constitutes engagement.

The friction between theory and practice isn't a bug. It's the only place where actual learning happens — not in the smooth operation of documented processes, but in the honest struggle with what doesn't translate.

What remains to be tested is whether we can design for this friction rather than trying to eliminate it.
