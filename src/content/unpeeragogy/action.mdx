### Reality

The Peeragogy Handbook tells you to start. Just begin — form a group, pick a project, iterate toward learning. But every maker who has tried this knows the first question that kills momentum: whose version of "working" wins? Three programmers start a collaborative coding project. Two want clean architecture. One wants shipping features. The handbook assumes they'll figure it out through dialogue and good intentions.

They don't.

Not because they lack commitment. Not because the tools are wrong. Not because peer learning is impossible — but because action without authority creates beautiful paralysis. Someone has to decide when the arguing stops and the building begins. The handbook's democratic idealism crashes into the human need for someone to call the shot when consensus fails. Every successful peer project I've seen has an unofficial decider who emerges from the chaos, usually the person willing to do the most unglamorous work.

The corporate world solved this with hierarchy. Open source solved it with benevolent dictators. Peeragogy refuses both solutions and offers instead the hope that peers will naturally align around shared purpose.

> What if the problem isn't that people won't take action — but that taking action without clear authority is the fastest way to kill peer collaboration?

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that action in peer contexts requires what traditional collaboration avoids: explicit acknowledgment of power. The programmer who merges pull requests holds power. The organizer who sets meeting agendas holds power. The person who maintains the documentation holds power. Peeragogy's emphasis on starting obscures this reality — that someone always decides what counts as progress.

What remains to be tested is whether peer groups can openly negotiate these power dynamics instead of pretending they don't exist. The useful friction may not be in the doing but in the honest conversation about who gets to define what "done" looks like.
