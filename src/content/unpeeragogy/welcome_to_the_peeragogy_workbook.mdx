### Reality

The Peeragogy Workbook opens with three exclamation points and the promise of a "fun, exciting world." This is how institutions talk when they've forgotten what learning feels like. Real peer learning happens when someone gets stuck — genuinely stuck — and another person who was stuck in the same place last week shows them the way through. Not because they read a manual about it.

I've watched dozens of learning communities die from over-documentation. The moment you need a 20-page guide to explain how peers should interact, you've already lost the peer part. What remains is a simulation of peer learning — all the structures, none of the organic friction that makes knowledge stick.

The workbook treats peer learning like a system to be implemented rather than a relationship to be earned.

> What if the real barrier to peer learning isn't lack of structure, but too much of it?

### Evidence That Supports

**about.gitlab.com** -- Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.


### Evidence That Complicates

**microbit.org** -- Educational technology projects with multiple stakeholders require structured governance with defined roles, not open peer participation.


### Evidence That Contradicts

*No case in the current corpus directly contradicts the pattern.*

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**apache.org** -- Meritocracy-based peer governance creates invisible barriers: those with more free time accumulate authority, creating a class system within the 'flat' organization.


### Synthesis

Corporate sponsors want governance boundaries because they need predictable outcomes. Educational technology projects demand defined roles because stakeholders require accountability. These aren't wrong impulses — they're institutional necessities that happen to kill the conditions under which peers actually learn from each other.

The evidence suggests that peer learning thrives in the spaces between formal structures, not within them. What remains to be tested is whether any documentation can preserve that quality of useful friction — or whether the act of writing it down transforms peer learning into something else entirely.
