### Reality

MOOCs promised connection through scale. Fifteen thousand strangers reading the same PDF on Tuesday, chatting in forums, forming "self-organized networks of learning." The language was seductive — decentralized, emergent, peer-to-peer. What actually happened was loneliness at industrial scale.

I watched three attempts at connectivist learning collapse in the same pattern. Week one: enthusiasm, introductions, shared resources. Week three: ghost towns. The same dozen people talking to each other while thousands lurked in silence. Not networks. Not communities. Chat rooms with a syllabus.

The human part got engineered out. No one knew who was responsible for what — not the content, not the pace, not even basic questions like "Is this working?" Connectivism assumes connections emerge naturally from proximity and shared interest. They don't. They require someone to hold the space, set the rhythm, pressure-test what's actually happening versus what we hope is happening.

### Synthesis

These failures suggest something uncomfortable about distributed learning theory. The models that survive aren't the most networked or the most open — they're the ones with clear ownership and defined roles. Someone facilitates. Someone decides. Someone holds the group accountable to its stated purpose.

This doesn't mean hierarchy wins over networks. It means networks need anchors — specific people responsible for specific functions, not because they have authority but because someone has to do the work. The sustainable peer learning I've seen operates more like jazz ensembles than flash mobs: improvised but not leaderless, open but not structureless.

What remains to be tested is whether we can design for useful friction rather than frictionless connection — systems that make it harder to disappear, not easier to join.
