### Claim

We’ve written a few papers about different aspects of the peeragogy

### Reality

Most academic papers about peer learning die in committee rooms where nobody practices peer learning. The authors write about collaboration while fighting over authorship order. They cite studies on open knowledge while hiding their data behind paywalls. The gap between what they recommend and how they work isn't hypocrisy — it's structural blindness.

I've watched teams spend months polishing papers about agile governance while their own decision-making process required three committee approvals to change a comma. Not broken process. Not poor execution. Not misaligned incentives. The paper itself becomes the performance of understanding, replacing the messy work of actually building what you claim to study.

The real test isn't peer review. It's whether the people who wrote about peer learning can actually learn from their peers when the paper gets torn apart.

> What if the paper that changes nothing is the one that perfectly describes how things should work?

### Evidence That Supports

**about.gitlab.com** -- Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.


### Evidence That Complicates

**docs.kernel.org** -- Formal peer assessment tags create accountability but also reveal review scarcity — the bottleneck shifts from writing code to getting it reviewed.


### Evidence That Contradicts

*No case in the current corpus directly contradicts the pattern.*

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**lists.debian.org** -- Regular leadership summaries in peer-governed projects create agenda-setting power — what gets summarized is what gets attention.


### Synthesis

These cases suggest that documentation and practice exist in different economies. A paper about governance boundaries may reduce friction precisely because it creates distance — the written rules become a buffer that prevents the human work of negotiating boundaries in real time. The formalization protects against the messiness it claims to solve.

Peer assessment systems reveal this same pattern. The accountability they create often exposes the scarcity they were meant to address. More review requirements don't generate more reviewers — they make visible how few people actually want to do the unglamorous work of assessment. The bottleneck shifts but doesn't disappear.

What remains to be tested is whether papers can resist their own tendency to substitute description for practice — or if the useful friction lies precisely in that gap between what we write and what we do.
