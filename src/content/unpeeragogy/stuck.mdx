### Claim

The Peeragogy Handbook identifies getting stuck as a problem of weak ties. The theory frames it as a network issue: the group lacks connections to diverse perspectives, so it cannot find a way forward. The proposed solution is to activate weak ties — reach beyond the immediate circle — and the bottleneck will dissolve through access to new information and resources.

### Reality

Getting stuck has two opposite causes. The wrong diagnosis makes it worse.

Sometimes a group is stuck because nobody has the authority to decide. Power is too distributed. Everyone can block, nobody can push. The solution would be to concentrate decision-making — but Peeragogy's flat governance ideals resist that move.

Other times a group is stuck because everybody gets to decide. Thousands of voices, each with equal weight, and nothing moves. ConstitutionDAO is the case: 17,000 token holders, one vote each, the DAO dissolved without executing its single purpose. Not because they lacked weak ties. Because they had too many decision-makers with equal power.

The weak ties framework is not wrong. It is incomplete. The bottleneck is not always who you know. Sometimes it is who can say yes — and who can say no.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if stuckness is not a network problem but an authority problem? Weak ties bring information. But information does not make a decision. Maybe the group is not stuck because it lacks connections. Maybe it is stuck because it lacks a mechanism to convert connections into decisions."*

### Evidence That Supports

**GitLab stewardship handbook** — Explicit documentation of who decides what. GitLab Inc and the community have clear boundaries: corporate strategy versus community contributions. When the boundary is clear, stuckness dissolves. The bottleneck was not weak ties. It was undefined authority.

**OpenStreetMap Data Working Group** — A formal dispute resolution body with binding authority. When informal consensus fails, the DWG decides. The cases are published, the reasoning is transparent, and the decision is final. Stuckness ends because someone — not everyone — has the last word.

### Evidence That Complicates

**ConstitutionDAO dissolution** — 17,000 token holders with equal voting rights. One purpose: buy a rare copy of the US Constitution at auction. The group could not coordinate effectively enough to execute. The DAO dissolved. Was this a failure of weak ties? Or a failure of decision-making structure? The evidence points to the second.

**Linux kernel Reviewed-by tag bottleneck** — The review process creates accountability, but the bottleneck shifts from writing code to getting it reviewed. Peer review does not distribute the work. It concentrates the waiting. The group is stuck not because of weak ties — but because the review process itself creates a new bottleneck.

### Evidence That Contradicts

*No case in the current corpus directly contradicts the pattern.*

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**Wikipedia Arbitration Committee** — A formal judicial body for disputes that informal consensus cannot resolve. It works. But it was created after years of failure, not before. Whether proactive authority design prevents stuckness better than reactive authority design is not tested by this case.

### Synthesis

What these cases suggest: diagnose the cause before applying the cure.

If nobody can decide, concentrate authority. Name a decision-maker. Give them the last word. The group may survive the loss of horizontality better than it survives paralysis.

If everybody can decide and nothing moves, reduce the number of decision-makers. Not all voices need veto power. Some voices can be consultative.

The weak ties framework is useful. But it may describe the symptom, not the cause. What remains to be tested: whether groups that explicitly design for decision-making authority are less likely to get stuck in the first place.
