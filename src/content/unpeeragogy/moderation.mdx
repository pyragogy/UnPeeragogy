### Reality

Wikipedia's arbitration committee handles about 30 cases per year. Real people with real names make binding decisions about content disputes that voluntary consensus couldn't resolve. They publish their reasoning. They enforce their rulings. The work is thankless, often brutal, and absolutely necessary for the encyclopedia to function.

Not peer consensus. Not community self-regulation. Not emergent wisdom of crowds. A small group with actual authority making hard calls that stick.

Most peer learning spaces avoid this reality. They prefer the fiction that good intentions and shared values will somehow moderate themselves. I've watched brilliant learning communities collapse because nobody wanted to be the person who says "no" — who removes the disruptive member, who enforces the participation standards, who makes the call that someone else's contribution isn't good enough.

The human part of moderation isn't keeping things smooth. It's being willing to create useful friction when smoothness becomes the enemy of learning.

> What if the real test of peer governance isn't how well it works when everyone agrees, but how quickly it can make hard decisions when they don't?

### Evidence That Supports

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that sustainable peer learning requires someone willing to hold authority that the community cannot collectively exercise. Not because peers are incapable of good judgment, but because judgment at scale demands institutional memory, consistent application, and the courage to disappoint people.

The Wikipedia model works because it separates different kinds of decisions — content editing remains radically open while dispute resolution becomes formally structured. Most learning communities try to keep everything at the same level of informality, which may explain why so many struggle with basic quality control.

What remains to be tested is whether peer learning spaces can develop this kind of institutional maturity without losing the collaborative energy that makes them valuable in the first place — or whether the price of sustainable moderation is always some loss of the peer ideal.
