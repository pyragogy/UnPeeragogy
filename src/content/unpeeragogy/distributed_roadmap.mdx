### Reality

The Peeragogy Handbook promises emergent roadmaps. Twenty-three patterns, each ending with "What's Next" steps that supposedly aggregate into collective direction. The theory sounds clean — peer networks naturally evolve toward shared goals through distributed sensing and response.

Watch any actual peer project try this. The Linux kernel didn't emerge from collective wisdom — Linus Torvalds made thousands of small dictatorial decisions until subsystem maintainers could carry the load. Wikipedia's early chaos required Jimmy Wales stepping in repeatedly until bureaucratic structures solidified. Not emergence. Governance.

The friction lives in the gap between sensing change and acting on it. Everyone can feel when something's broken. Few will name it publicly. Fewer will volunteer to fix it. The roadmap that "emerges" is usually the path of least resistance — which means the path that avoids the hardest conversations.

> What if the refusal to plan is itself a plan — one that protects existing power structures by making change feel impossible?

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that emergence may be a luxury available only after someone has already done the structural work. The transition from benevolent dictator to community governance requires years of deliberate institution-building, not patient waiting for consensus to crystallize.

The evidence points toward a different kind of distributed leadership — one that rotates decision-making authority rather than diffusing it into collective paralysis. What remains untested is whether peer networks can develop the appetite for this kind of useful friction — the willingness to disagree productively rather than defer endlessly to the wisdom of crowds that never quite materializes.
