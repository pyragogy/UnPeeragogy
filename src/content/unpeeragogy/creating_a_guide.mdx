"Established ideas have knowledge cartography challenges for newcomers." "Deciphering a subway map in a foreign city."

The metaphor is perfect: entering an existing project is like deciphering a subway map in a language you don't know.

**Why it matters:** Creating a guide for newcomers is an act of kindness. But the guide itself becomes a barrier. Because to understand the guide, you must already understand the project. And to understand the project, you must read the guide. It's a circular trap that veterans don't see because they've been inside it too long.

The problem isn't creating the guide. The problem is that the guide is written by people who know the project. People who know the project don't know what a newcomer doesn't know. The obvious questions aren't written because for veterans they're obvious. The guide explains the hard things. But newcomers are stuck on the easy things. The guide doesn't serve them. It serves the veterans' need to feel organized.

**What to do instead:** A guide works if it's short, if it says what to do on day one, and if it gives an immediate task. It doesn't work if it explains history, vision, organizational structure. On day one, the newcomer doesn't need to understand everything. They need to *do* something. The guide to doing is different from the guide to understanding. Make the right one.

> **Perturbator:** *"Your newcomer guide is 14 pages long. It's detailed. It's complete. It's also why newcomers don't arrive. No one reads 14 pages before participating. The perfect guide is one page. If you can't sum up your project in one page, you don't know what your project is."*
