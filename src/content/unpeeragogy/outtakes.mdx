### The Assumption

The Peeragogy Handbook's Outtakes section presents curated material while omitting conflicts and arguments, preferring comfort over uncomfortable truths rather than documenting the real fights and disagreements that occurred.

### Operational Reality

Every peer project has a cutting room floor. The design sprint where two team members stopped speaking. The funding proposal that revealed who really controlled the roadmap. The community vote that was decided before the votes were cast.

The handbook's Outtakes section curates these moments into neat lessons. It is not dishonest — it is editing. But editing decisions are governance decisions, and treating them as editorial neuters their political weight.

GitLab's stewardship handbook does not hide its governance conflicts. It documents corporate-community boundaries explicitly, naming what cannot be negotiated. OpenStreetMap's Data Working Group publishes its dispute resolutions with reasoning attached. Wikipedia's Arbitration Committee creates a permanent record of community conflicts that consensus failed to resolve. The handbook's outtakes could do the same. Instead, they offer comfortable reflection. And comfort is the enemy of learning when the lesson is about power.

WebAssembly shows that sanitizing governance trade-offs creates false expectations about peer participation. Wikipedia ArbCom demonstrates that even the most transparent peer community requires a formal conflict layer — the outtakes are governance, not editorial.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if curating out the fights is like teaching surgery without showing the blood — and the outtakes section is where the real manual should begin?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [GitLab Stewardship Handbook](https://about.gitlab.com/handbook/company/stewardship/) — Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects. Documentation including governance conflicts prepares participants for real tensions.
- [OpenStreetMap Data Working Group](https://wiki.osmfoundation.org/wiki/Data_Working_Group) — Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts. Publishing dispute reasoning teaches governance more effectively than curated lessons.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [WebAssembly Community Group](https://www.w3.org/community/webassembly/) — Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes. Sanitizing governance trade-offs creates false expectations about peer participation.
- [Wikipedia Arbitration Committee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitration_Committee_(Wikimedia)) — Permanent records of community conflicts that consensus failed to resolve. Even the most transparent peer community requires a formal conflict layer — the outtakes are governance, not editorial.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Documentation works when it includes explicit governance boundaries and formal dispute resolution mechanisms with published reasoning — documenting conflicts prepares participants for real governance tensions.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not curate governance conflicts out of peer pattern documentation — sanitized documentation does not prepare participants for the governance tensions they will inevitably encounter.

### Epistemic Synthesis

Every peer project has a cutting room floor. The sponsor who documents governance boundaries does not hide its conflicts — it names them explicitly. OpenStreetMap's dispute resolution publishes reasoning. Wikipedia's ArbCom creates a permanent record of conflicts that consensus failed to resolve. The handbook's outtakes could do the same. Instead, they offer comfortable reflection. And comfort is the enemy of learning when the lesson is about power.
