### Claim

Ideas are not born fully formed. They arrive unannounced, often in the wrong room, at the wrong time, wearing the wrong clothes. You recognise them not by their clarity but by their insistence — a quiet tug that doesn't stop, a question that refuses to be answered by any existing framework.

### Reality

The corporate sponsor writes a check. The community writes the code. Between these two facts lies the entire problem of peer governance in open-core projects. I've watched teams spend months crafting governance documents that read like constitutional conventions — elaborate voting procedures, rotating leadership roles, consensus protocols that would make the UN jealous. Then the sponsor's quarterly review arrives and half the roadmap gets rewritten over a weekend.

Not because anyone is dishonest. Not because the documents were poorly written. Not because the community lacks commitment. Because governance boundaries written on paper dissolve the moment real money and real deadlines enter the room. The explicit documentation everyone celebrates as transparency becomes a kind of theater — performed for the community while the actual decisions happen in Slack channels they can't see.

> > What if the foreword that doesn't announce is actually the only honest way to begin — admitting that we don't know where this leads, only that something insists on being followed?"*

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that peer governance may require a different kind of honesty about power. The scholarly communication systems that demand hierarchies to function expose something uncomfortable — that pure egalitarianism might be a luxury only systems without real consequences can afford. When tenure decisions hang in the balance, someone has to decide what counts as legitimate peer review.

The executive who mandates peer governance creates a paradox that won't resolve through better process design. Authority that declares its own obsolescence doesn't disappear — it just becomes harder to see, more difficult to challenge. What remains to be tested is whether genuine peer governance emerges only when no one is trying to create it, when the conditions arise from necessity rather than intention.
