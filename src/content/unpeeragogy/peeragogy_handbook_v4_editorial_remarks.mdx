## Summary

Editorial remarks become governance documentation when communities can't admit their structure is broken. GitLab documents stewardship boundaries explicitly — Wikipedia buries arbitration in meta-pages. The difference is whether you treat organizational debt as feature or bug.

## Evidenze Grounded

### 🔹 Confirming (2)

**Caso 1 — GitLab stewardship handbook**
- **Fenomeno:** Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.
- **Fonte:** [https://about.gitlab.com/handbook/company/stewardship/](https://about.gitlab.com/handbook/company/stewardship/)

**Caso 2 — Wikipedia Good Article process**
- **Fenomeno:** Peer assessment with transparent criteria and independent reviewers creates sustainable quality tiers in open communities.
- **Fonte:** [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Good_articles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Good_articles)

### 🔸 Complicating (2)

- **Fenomeno:** Peer production communities develop formal judicial bodies when informal consensus fails, contradicting the assumption that peers can self-govern without hierarchy.
- **Fonte:** [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitration_Committee_(Wikimedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitration_Committee_(Wikimedia))

**Caso 2 — Wikipedia Featured Article Candidate process**
- **Fenomeno:** Peer assessment systems in open communities produce quality hierarchies that conflict with flat peer governance ideals.
- **Fonte:** [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Featured_article_candidates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Featured_article_candidates)

## Euristiche Operative

### Positive Rule

> This pattern works when governance boundaries are explicitly documented and quality hierarchies are transparently acknowledged. GitLab's stewardship handbook and Wikipedia's Good Article process show that explicit documentation of roles and transparent assessment criteria create sustainable peer governance systems.

### Negative Rule

> Do not apply this pattern when governance structures are informal or buried in meta-commentary. Wikipedia's Arbitration Committee shows that peer communities develop formal judicial bodies when informal consensus fails, contradicting the assumption that peers can self-govern without acknowledged hierarchy.

## Sintesi Obliqua

The pattern claims transparent documentation reduces governance friction in peer communities. Partial verification — it works when boundaries are explicit, not buried. GitLab's stewardship handbook and Wikipedia's Good Article process show that documented roles create sustainable systems, but Wikipedia's Arbitration Committee and hidden editorial asides reveal that communities develop shadow governance when formal structures can't handle conflict. The real mechanism isn't transparency — it's whether you're willing to name power structures out loud. Most communities prefer the mess to the admission.
