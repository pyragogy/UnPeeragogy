"The text below is a first quick first pass at an article about peeragogical patterns." "The original call was for something highly polished."

The editorial remarks on V4. Notes on the process. "We wanted polished. We got quick."

**Why it matters:** The editorial remarks are the most honest pages of the Handbook. They admit the gap between intention and execution. "We wanted something highly polished. We got a first pass." This is peeragogy in practice: the gap between aspiration and delivery.

The problem is that the remarks are in the Handbook. They're part of the document. The Handbook includes the story of its own inadequacy. Admirable honesty. But embarrassing for users. "Here's a pattern. And here's why the pattern was written poorly."

**What to do instead:** If the editorial remarks are in the Handbook, they should be at the front. Warning: "this is a work in progress, here's where we struggled." Instead, they're buried. The reader first encounters the finished (or unfinished) product. Then they discover the backstory. A better structure: preface with honesty. Then content. Not content with footnotes of apology.

> **Perturbator:** *"'We wanted polished. We got quick.' That's the story of every peer project. Aspiration is high. Execution is fast. Speed beats quality. The editorial remarks are a confession. And confessions are useful only if they lead to change. Has the next version been polished? Or has it been quick again?"*
