### Claim

Authors: Miguel Ángel Álvarez Pérez and Elisa Armendáriz

### Reality

Miguel Ángel Álvarez Pérez taught at a technical school in Mexico City where students arrived knowing more about phones than teachers knew about teaching. The SWATS program — Students With Abilities in Technology — was supposed to flip this dynamic. Students would become peer tutors, teaching video editing and programming to classmates while teachers stepped back to facilitate. The technology was ready. The students were capable. The institutional framework existed.

What happened instead was predictable human friction. Students who became SWATS gained status but lost peers — suddenly they were "helpers" rather than collaborators. Teachers felt displaced but remained responsible for grades and discipline. The technology amplified existing hierarchies rather than dissolving them. Not a failure of tools. Not a failure of intention. A failure to account for the social weight that any learning technology carries into existing power structures.

The program worked when it ignored its own theory — when SWATS stopped being designated experts and became troubleshooters who happened to know things others needed.

> What if the most sophisticated collaboration technology is still just an expensive way to avoid the harder work of changing how people relate to each other?

### Synthesis

The SWATS case suggests that peer learning technologies succeed not when they eliminate hierarchy but when they make existing power dynamics visible enough to negotiate. The students who thrived weren't those with the best technical skills — they were those who could read social cues well enough to offer help without claiming authority.

This points toward a different design principle: technology that exposes rather than obscures the human dynamics at play. The governance frameworks that sustain complex peer production over decades — Apache, Linux, Wikipedia — don't eliminate hierarchy but make it explicit and contestable. They create useful friction rather than seamless collaboration.

What remains untested is whether learning communities can develop similar governance literacy — the ability to recognize when peer relationships need structure and when structure needs to get out of the way.
