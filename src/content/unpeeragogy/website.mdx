### Reality

The peeragogy.org website sits there. A handbook, some updates, the usual architecture of information delivery. You visit, you read, you leave. The two-way street promised in the theory remains a one-way broadcast — polite, organized, dead.

Not a forum. Not a workspace. Not a learning laboratory where peers actually meet and struggle with ideas together. The website functions as a repository, and repositories don't teach you how to learn with others. They teach you how to consume alone.

Static websites can't demonstrate peeragogy because peeragogy isn't information — it's the friction between people who disagree about what they're building together.

> What if the website became the place where the method fails publicly, where visitors see peers arguing about whether something actually works?

### Evidence That Supports

**p2pu.org** -- Peer learning models with defined facilitator roles and institutional partnerships are more sustainable than purely peer-driven learning.

**about.gitlab.com** -- Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.


### Evidence That Complicates

**p2pu.org** -- Peer learning communities inevitably face credentialing and quality assurance crises that require either hierarchy or external validation frameworks.


### Evidence That Contradicts

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**researchgate.net** -- The divide in open education between peer-governed and institution-governed models shows that peer governance has known scalability limits.


### Synthesis

These cases suggest that peer learning needs visible governance boundaries, not invisible ones. The sustainable models don't pretend hierarchy doesn't exist — they make the power structures explicit so peers can work within them or push against them deliberately.

What remains untested is whether a website can hold this kind of honest friction. Whether it can show the work of learning together, not just the results. The credentialing crisis doesn't go away when you ignore it — it surfaces as the quiet death of communities that can't admit what they don't know how to do.
