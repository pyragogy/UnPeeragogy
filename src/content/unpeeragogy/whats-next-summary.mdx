## Summary

Roadmaps collect good intentions. Theory says distributed planning emerges from collective input. Practice shows that without explicit ownership, next-steps become archaeological artifacts — preserved but never executed.

## Euristiche Operative

### Positive Rule

> This pattern works when governance boundaries are explicitly documented before summary creation. GitLab's stewardship handbook shows that clear documentation of decision-making authority and resource commitment processes enables productive forward-looking planning in collaborative projects.

### Negative Rule

> Do not create forward-looking summaries without explicit governance boundaries. The existing 'What's Next' content shows that vague summaries produce decision-stagnation and consensus-paralysis when authority and ownership remain undefined.

## Sintesi Obliqua

The pattern claims distributed roadmaps emerge from collecting individual contributions. Partial verification — it works when governance boundaries exist before the summary gets written. GitLab's stewardship handbook demonstrates that explicit documentation of decision authority enables forward momentum, while Debian's leadership newsletters show how summarization creates agenda-setting power. But the ArbCom case reveals the real mechanism: roadmaps without owners become museums where everyone can visit but nobody lives. The missing piece isn't coordination. It's the quiet authority to say no.
