### Reality

The Peeragogy handbook ends with a distributed roadmap — twenty-three "What's Next" steps collected from individual patterns, no owners assigned, no timeline attached. It reads like archaeology of ambition. Each step assumes someone else will pick it up.

I've seen this movie. The community garden that mapped thirty raised beds but never bought seeds. The maker collective that documented every tool they'd need but couldn't agree on who unlocks the space. Not laziness — structural optimism. The belief that good documentation generates action.

The friction lives in the gap between "easy to add" and "easy to sustain." Making contribution frictionless sounds generous. It's actually expensive. Someone absorbs the coordination cost, the context-switching, the work of keeping twenty-three threads alive in their head. That someone burns out quietly.

Roadmaps work when they're owned, not when they're distributed.

> What if the most honest roadmap is the one that admits which futures we've already abandoned?

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**lists.debian.org** -- Regular leadership summaries in peer-governed projects create agenda-setting power — what gets summarized is what gets attention.

**en.wikipedia.org** -- The Wikipedia Arbitration Committee (ArbCom) case in 2021 highlighted conflicts in community governance, where differing views on content moderation led to heated debates. The theoretical pattern posits that shared governance leads to consensus, yet this case illustrates how diverse opinions can complicate decision-making processes, resulting in protracted disputes.


### Synthesis

The corporate sponsor documentation suggests something useful — explicit boundaries reduce friction, but only when someone has authority to enforce them. The Peeragogy roadmap distributes both the work and the authority to prioritize it. 

This may work for knowledge that wants to be preserved, not knowledge that wants to be enacted. A handbook can survive distributed maintenance. A project needs someone willing to say no to good ideas, to kill healthy branches so the trunk can grow.

The question isn't whether distributed roadmaps can work — it's whether they can resist becoming monuments to what we meant to build.
