### Reality

The Peeragogy handbook lists seven case studies. I've read them all. Three describe projects that no longer exist. Two present idealized versions of messy realities. One admits failure but frames it as learning. The last one — "Peeragogy in Action" — is a meta-case about writing case studies.

Not one shows the governance crisis that killed SWATS. Not one documents how 5PH1NX's peer model collapsed when members needed actual credentials. The Pro Vice-Chancellor meeting gets sanitized into "productive dialogue" — no mention of the institutional resistance that followed. These aren't case studies. They're success theater performed for an audience that wants to believe peer learning works without friction.

The human part gets edited out.

> What if case studies are where peer learning theories go to hide from reality?

### Synthesis

> What if case studies are where peer learning theories go to hide from reality?


The pattern suggests something uncomfortable about how we document peer learning. When projects succeed, we credit the peer model. When they fail, we blame implementation or external factors — never the model itself. This creates a literature where peer learning can't lose, which means it can't learn.

Real case studies would show the governance transitions that never happened. The credentialing crises that forced hierarchy back in. The moments when peer accountability broke down and someone had to make decisions alone. These aren't failures of peer learning — they're the conditions under which any learning model operates.

What remains untested is whether we can build theory that holds when the stories stop being false.
