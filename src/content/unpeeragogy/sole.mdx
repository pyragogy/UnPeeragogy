### Claim

Self-Organizing Learning Environments (SOLE) are based on the premise that groups can naturally explore questions through emergent self-organization, with minimal external structure or facilitation.

### Reality

Jan Herder runs learning experiments in Amsterdam. He watches groups form, struggle, dissolve. The pattern repeats: enthusiastic launch, gradual drift, silent abandonment. Not because people lose interest — because they never built the infrastructure to hold that interest.

The handbook promises "emerging structure" and "ripple effects." But structure doesn't emerge from good intentions. David Preston's case study reveals the gap between theory and practice — peer learning needs someone to organize the peers before they can organize themselves. The Wikipedia model works because Jimmy Wales made the hard decisions about editing policies and dispute resolution. The successful peer learning groups Herder observes all have one thing in common: someone did the unglamorous work of creating frameworks before the peers arrived.

This isn't failure of self-organization. It's the honest prerequisite nobody wants to discuss.

> What if the most peer-driven learning happens only after someone builds the container strong enough to hold the chaos?

### Evidence That Supports

**p2pu.org** -- Peer learning models with defined facilitator roles and institutional partnerships are more sustainable than purely peer-driven learning.

**wiki.osmfoundation.org** -- Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot.


### Evidence That Complicates

**w3.org** -- Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes for peer participation.


### Evidence That Contradicts

*No case in the current corpus directly contradicts the pattern.*

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**ietf.org** -- Rough consensus governance only works when backed by hierarchical escalation paths; pure consensus without appeals fails.


### Synthesis

The evidence suggests a pattern that complicates our romance with pure peer governance. Formal dispute resolution mechanisms and defined facilitator roles create sustainability that consensus-driven models cannot achieve. But lighter governance trades authority for speed — different failure modes, not better ones.

What remains untested is whether we can design learning containers that become genuinely self-organizing over time. The tension may not be between structure and freedom, but between premature structure and patient infrastructure — building strong enough foundations that peers can eventually tear down the scaffolding.
