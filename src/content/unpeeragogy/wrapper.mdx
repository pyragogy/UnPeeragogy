### Reality

Fabrizio watches the Slack notifications pile up. The community coordinator sends daily digest emails nobody reads. The project manager schedules weekly check-ins that half the contributors skip. The documentation lead maintains wikis that grow stale between updates. Three people now spend their time keeping everyone else informed about work they could be doing instead.

This is the wrapper pattern in practice — not elegant delegation, but bureaucratic drift. The theory promises someone will "manage the public interface" and "keep participants updated." What actually happens is that managing becomes the work, and the work becomes secondary to its management.

Not coordination. Not facilitation. Not even leadership.

Administrative capture.

> > What if the person managing your project's interface is the main obstacle to getting anything done?"*

### Synthesis

The evidence suggests something uncomfortable about peer projects at scale. Explicit governance boundaries work — when you can draw them cleanly. Defined facilitator roles create sustainability — when the facilitators stay focused on facilitation rather than control. But every wrapper role carries the seeds of its own expansion.

The honest tension here isn't between chaos and order. It's between useful friction and administrative drag. The wrapper who keeps essential information flowing serves the project. The wrapper who becomes the information bottleneck — who must approve, review, or route every decision — serves only the wrapper role itself.

What remains to be tested is whether any coordination role can resist this drift toward administrative capture, or whether the wrapper pattern simply describes how peer projects die by a thousand process improvements.
