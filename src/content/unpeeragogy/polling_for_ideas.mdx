### Claim

Polling for Ideas can happen at many junctures in a peer learning

### Reality

A software collective spent three months polling contributors about which framework to adopt. Weekly surveys, ranked voting, endless Slack threads weighing technical debt against learning curves. The results shifted with each poll — React one week, Vue the next, back to vanilla JavaScript when someone shared a Medium post about bloat. Six months later, they were still using the legacy jQuery codebase because no decision stuck.

Not consensus. Not democracy. Not wisdom of crowds.

The polling revealed what everyone already suspected but wouldn't say directly — nobody wanted to take responsibility for a choice that might fail. Each new poll became a way to defer the decision while appearing collaborative. The process had become the product.

> What if polling for ideas isn't about gathering intelligence, but about taking the temperature of a group's willingness to commit?

### Synthesis

The software collective's endless surveys suggest something uncomfortable about peer learning environments. Polling often functions as a status check on group cohesion rather than genuine knowledge gathering. When a community keeps asking the same questions in different forms, they may be testing whether anyone will step forward to own an outcome.

This doesn't make polling useless — it makes it diagnostic. The rhythm of questions reveals where confidence lives and where it doesn't. A group that polls frequently for direction may need structure more than input. The useful friction emerges when someone finally stops asking what everyone thinks and starts demonstrating what might work.
