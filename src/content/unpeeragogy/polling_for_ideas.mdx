## Summary

Polling measures preferences without creating accountability for outcomes. Theory says ask the crowd — practice shows you need someone to count votes and enforce results. The mechanism works when authority structures exist to act on what gets measured.

## Euristiche Operative

### Positive Rule

> This pattern works when polling lacks binding authority and transparent criteria. Informal polls without enforcement mechanisms do lead to decision-stagnation, as the original pattern describes, because they measure preferences without creating accountability for outcomes.

### Negative Rule

> Do not apply this pattern when formal governance structures with binding authority exist. OpenStreetMap's Data Working Group shows that peer groups can make accountable decisions when they have clear dispute resolution mechanisms and published reasoning requirements.

## Sintesi Obliqua

The pattern claims informal polling leads to decision-stagnation because groups don't know answers in advance. Partial verification — but only when polling lacks binding authority. OpenStreetMap's Data Working Group and Wikipedia's Good Article process both use peer input with enforcement mechanisms and transparent criteria, proving groups can make accountable decisions when structured properly. WebAssembly's lighter governance complicates this by showing some communities deliberately choose informal modes. What's missing is the recognition that polling isn't broken — it's just a measurement tool. The real question is whether anyone has the authority to act on what gets counted.
