### Reality

Gigi Johnson's co-learning groups fall apart on schedule. Three months in, half the participants stop showing up to calls. Six months later, the Slack goes quiet except for automated reminders. The organizers blame themselves — maybe the content wasn't engaging enough, maybe they needed better onboarding, maybe lurkers just aren't committed.

Not a motivation problem. Not a content problem. Not a people problem.

A governance problem.

The groups that survive messy co-learning don't eliminate lurking — they design for it. Wikipedia works because lurkers can edit anonymously without joining anything. Open source thrives because you can contribute a single bug fix without attending meetings. The successful peer learning communities Johnson studies all share one trait: they make participation useful at every level of engagement, from drive-by comments to deep collaboration.

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that "messy with lurkers" isn't a problem to solve but a design constraint to embrace. The communities that last build governance systems that work for people who show up once, people who show up sometimes, and people who show up always. They document decisions in public channels rather than private calls. They create multiple entry points rather than single onboarding funnels.

What remains untested is whether this principle scales beyond digital communities. The tension between inclusive participation and efficient decision-making — that friction may be exactly what keeps peer learning honest rather than performative.
