"'Messy with lurkers' is a pattern where participants stay on the sidelines." "Lurking often emerges when it's unclear how to contribute."

The Handbook describes the pattern: "messy with lurkers." A group where some work, some watch, and everything is a bit chaotic.

**Why it matters:** "Messy with lurkers" isn't a problem to solve. It's the normal state of any peer group. In almost every group, 10% produces, 20% occasionally participates, 70% watches. This isn't a failure of peeragogy. It's a power distribution. The Handbook sees lurking as a problem. Practice knows lurking is a resource. The lurkers are the audience. Without audience, the producers produce for no one.

The problem isn't lurking. It's that the group calls lurking a problem instead of using it. Lurkers observe. They notice things that producers don't. They ask questions that insiders can't see. And sometimes, a lurker becomes a producer. The transition is unpredictable. We can't force it. We can only create conditions for it.

**What to do instead:** Don't try to eliminate lurking. Design for it. Create ways for lurkers to contribute that are lighter than producing. A comment. A reaction. A signal. And accept that most will remain lurkers. The group needs lurkers. Without them, the producers are alone. And lone producers burn out.

> **Perturbator:** *"You have 10 active people and 40 lurkers. You're worried about the lurkers. Don't be. The lurkers are your audience. Without them, the 10 active people would be performing in an empty room. The problem isn't that lurkers don't contribute. It's that active people don't know how to value them. Stop chasing lurkers. They're already giving you attention. That's a contribution."*
