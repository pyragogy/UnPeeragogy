### The Assumption

Groups should regularly review their patterns to identify which ones are working and which ones need adjustment, using this reflection to improve their collaborative processes.

### Operational Reality

The IETF has been auditing its working group processes for decades. Rough consensus, running code, working group chairs — the patterns are documented, reviewed, refined. Yet the same complaints surface every year: decision paralysis, dominated discussions, chairs with too much power.

Not because the audit process failed. Not because the patterns are wrong. Because auditing without authority produces recommendations without implementation.

OpenStreetMap's Data Working Group does not just audit — it resolves. Binding decisions with published reasoning. Fedora's Engineering Steering Committee does not just review — it governs. Elected representatives with defined scope and transparent records. The difference between pattern audit and governance is the authority to implement what the audit discovers. Most peer groups audit beautifully and change nothing.

Drupal shows that auditing patterns without implementation authority produces documentation of dysfunction without resolution. IETF demonstrates that pattern auditing without implementation authority produces repeated recommendations without change.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if auditing without authority is just organized complaining with better documentation — and the audit becomes the thing that replaces the change it was supposed to produce?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [OpenStreetMap Data Working Group](https://wiki.osmfoundation.org/wiki/Data_Working_Group) — Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts. Audit bodies with enforcement authority produce real change.
- [Fedora Engineering Steering Committee (FESCo)](https://docs.fedoraproject.org/en-US/fesco/) — Elected technical steering committees with defined scope and transparent records provide sustainable peer governance. Elected bodies with decision-making authority can audit and implement simultaneously.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [IETF Working Group Rough Consensus](https://www.ietf.org/about/) — Rough consensus governance only works when backed by hierarchical escalation paths; pure consensus without appeals fails. Pattern auditing without implementation authority produces repeated recommendations without change.
- [Drupal Governance](https://www.drupal.org/governance) — Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model required years of structural work. Auditing patterns without implementation authority produces documentation of dysfunction without resolution.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Pattern audit works when the auditing body has binding decision-making authority and published reasoning processes — formal authority structures with transparency allow audit and implementation simultaneously.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not apply pattern audit when the auditing group lacks authority to implement changes — consensus-only audit fails without appeal processes, creating decision paralysis instead of improvement.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The difference between pattern audit and governance is the authority to implement what the audit discovers. Peer groups that function do not separate these functions — OpenStreetMap and Fedora show that the same bodies that analyze patterns also have the power to change them. The IETF shows the cost of separating them: accurate audits that produce recommendations without implementation. Circular auditing is a risk of every reflective system — observation becomes a substitute for action.
