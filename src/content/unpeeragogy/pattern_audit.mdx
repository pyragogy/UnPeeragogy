### Claim

As a collection of patterns grows, some of them may lose relevance.

### Reality

# REALITY

A startup founder shows me their team retrospectives. Forty-three action items from the last six months. Color-coded spreadsheet. Progress tracking. Zero items actually closed. The founder keeps scheduling more retrospectives to figure out why the retrospectives aren't working.

Not broken process. Not missing tools. Not insufficient buy-in. The audit exists to avoid the authority to act. When you can measure everything, you never have to decide anything. The documentation becomes the work — safer than the friction of actually changing what doesn't serve.

I watch open source maintainers burn out while their communities debate governance frameworks in hundred-comment GitHub threads. The patterns get audited endlessly while the software rots. Someone always has a better process to propose, another template to fill out, one more stakeholder to consult before anyone can touch the actual problem.

> What if the urge to audit patterns is just the fear of admitting some experiments should die?

### Evidence That Supports

**wiki.osmfoundation.org** -- Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot.

**docs.fedoraproject.org** -- Elected technical steering committees with defined scope and transparent records provide sustainable peer governance for large distributions.


### Evidence That Complicates

**ietf.org** -- Rough consensus governance only works when backed by hierarchical escalation paths; pure consensus without appeals fails.

**drupal.org** -- Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model requires years of structural work, not organic emergence.

### Synthesis

# 
These cases suggest that pattern auditing may serve institutional anxiety more than institutional health. The formal mechanisms — dispute resolution, steering committees, documented processes — work when they carry real authority to act on what they discover. The endless retrospectives and governance debates work as elaborate delay tactics when no one wants to wield that authority.

What remains to be tested is whether pattern collections can survive honest pruning. The useful friction isn't in the auditing process itself but in the willingness to kill what you've built when it stops serving. Most audits discover problems everyone already knows — the question becomes who has permission to act on inconvenient truths.
