## Summary

Roadmaps fail when they promise coordination without naming coordinators. The pattern assumes shared goals emerge from discussion — but practice shows you need explicit ownership before the planning starts. Without designated roles, even transparent processes become wish lists with deadlines.

## Euristiche Operative

### Positive Rule

> This pattern works when governance boundaries are explicit and roles are defined before planning begins. Debian's stable release process and GitLab's stewardship handbook demonstrate that transparent policies with designated responsibilities produce reliable long-term outcomes in peer governance contexts.

### Negative Rule

> Do not apply this pattern when the group exceeds informal coordination limits or when domain complexity requires specialized decision-making. Wikipedia's ArbCom conflicts (2021) show that shared governance produces protracted disputes when opinions diverge without clear resolution mechanisms.

## Sintesi Obliqua

The pattern claims peer governance works through transparent policies and shared milestones. Partial verification — but only when group size stays within direct coordination limits. Debian's stable releases and GitLab's stewardship handbook prove explicit boundaries work, while Wikipedia's ArbCom conflicts and failed peer startups show what happens when domain complexity exceeds informal governance capacity. The real mechanism isn't transparency or shared goals. It's the unglamorous work of naming who decides what when consensus breaks.
