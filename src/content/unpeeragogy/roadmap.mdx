### Claim

This pattern shows how your group can define the scope of their project

### Reality

Three years ago, a distributed team started building an open learning platform. They had a roadmap — eighteen months, four major releases, clear milestones. The roadmap lived in a shared document that anyone could edit. Six months in, the document had grown to forty-seven pages of features, timeline adjustments, and competing priorities. The original eighteen-month plan stretched to three years. Half the original contributors had moved on.

Not a failure of planning. Not a failure of vision. Not a failure of commitment — a failure to understand that roadmaps in peer contexts don't work like roadmaps in hierarchies. When everyone can edit the destination, the map becomes the territory. The Wikipedia Arbitration Committee learned this in 2021 when governance debates consumed more energy than content creation. Peer governance works until it meets the friction of actual decisions.

I've watched teams spend months perfecting roadmaps that never survive contact with the messy reality of distributed work. The roadmap becomes a beautiful artifact of intentions — detailed, collaborative, and ultimately irrelevant.

> What if the roadmap's real function isn't to predict the future but to make visible who has the authority to change course when predictions fail?

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that roadmaps in peer contexts serve a different function than in traditional project management. They may be less about scheduling work and more about exposing the hidden hierarchies that emerge when decisions must be made under pressure. The open education divide between peer-governed and institution-governed models reveals what remains to be tested — whether distributed authority can coexist with the bounded commitments that complex projects require.

The useful friction here isn't between planning and spontaneity. It's between the democratic ideal of shared ownership and the practical reality that someone must decide when the roadmap needs to change — and someone else must accept that decision.
