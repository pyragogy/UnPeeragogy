### Claim

### Reality

The Wikipedia administrator who burned out after three years told me she quit the day she realized she was doing other people's work for free. Not editing articles — that was the joy. The endless mediation between editors who couldn't agree on a comma placement. The cleanup of vandalism that happened faster than she could revert it. The careful diplomacy required to keep productive contributors from leaving over personality conflicts.

She wasn't carrying the project's intellectual load. She was carrying its emotional overhead — the friction that accumulates when humans try to coordinate without hierarchy. Not the creative struggle of building something together. The exhausting work of preventing that something from falling apart.

Every peer production system discovers this eventually. Someone ends up carrying what everyone else won't.

> What if the people who talk most about distributed leadership are the ones least willing to distribute the actual work?

SYNTHESIS

The cases suggest that carrying capacity becomes an excuse precisely when communities mistake coordination work for optional overhead. The administrator wasn't failing at peer production — she was succeeding at keeping it functional while others focused on the interesting parts. External pressure forces governance changes because someone finally acknowledges what informal consensus costs in human labor.

What remains untested is whether peer communities can learn to recognize this carrying work before it burns people out. The pattern may be less about managing individual stress and more about making visible the invisible labor that keeps collaboration possible — the work that someone always ends up doing alone.
