### Claim

Actually, living beings are never really in stasis. It just sometimes

### Reality

Three months into the project, the weekly calls had become a ritual of polite paralysis. Same faces, same agenda items circling back like debris in a drain. The facilitator would ask for volunteers — silence. Someone would suggest forming a working group to explore the suggestion — nods all around, no names offered. The Google Doc grew longer with bracketed questions nobody wanted to answer.

Not deadlock. Not creative tension. Not productive disagreement.

This was something else — the peculiar death that comes when a group mistakes motion for movement. Everyone showing up, everyone participating, everyone agreeing that something should happen while ensuring nothing does. The handbook calls it stasis, but that misses the active quality of the thing. Real stasis would be honest. This was group suicide disguised as collaboration.

I watched it happen again last week in a different context entirely. A neighborhood association, eighteen months of meetings about the crosswalk. Same pattern — consensus as a weapon against decision, process as a moat around action.

> What if the problem isn't that groups get stuck, but that they choose stuckness as a way to avoid the risk of being wrong together?

### Synthesis

The cases suggest something unsettling about peer governance. External pressure — a deadline, a crisis, a competitor — can force movement where internal deliberation cannot. The crosswalk association finally acted when the city threatened to withdraw funding. The project team came alive only when the client grew impatient.

This points to a design flaw in how we think about consensus and collaboration. We treat agreement as the goal rather than a tool for testing ideas against reality. The result is systems that optimize for comfort over capability — structures that protect the group from the friction of actually deciding something that matters.

What remains untested is whether peer groups can build useful friction into their own processes, or whether they will always need external pressure to escape the gravity well of endless consultation.
