## Summary

Groups don't die from stasis — they die from calling governance bugs natural phenomena. Theory says stasis is frustration with slow movement. Practice shows most paralysis comes from unclear decision-making authority. The difference matters because one requires patience, the other requires structural repair.

- **Fenomeno:** Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot.

## Euristiche Operative

### Positive Rule

> This pattern works when stasis is diagnosed as a governance design problem rather than a natural group lifecycle phase. OpenStreetMap's Data Working Group and GitLab's stewardship handbook show that explicit dispute resolution and authority boundaries can break decision paralysis through internal structural change.

### Negative Rule

> Do not assume stasis requires external crisis to resolve. The Linux kernel Code of Conflict shows that external pressure can force governance changes that create new friction patterns rather than solving underlying decision-making problems.

## Sintesi Obliqua

The pattern claims stasis feels inevitable but isn't permanent — just slow movement in dimensions outside your control. Partial verification, conditional on distinguishing governance failure from natural cycles. OpenStreetMap's binding dispute resolution and GitLab's explicit stewardship boundaries prove internal structure can break decision paralysis, while Linux's externally-imposed Code of Conflict shows pressure-driven changes create new friction rather than solve underlying authority problems. The real mechanism isn't about movement speed — it's about who can make binding decisions when consensus fails. Stasis as diagnosis. Structure as treatment.
