### Claim

Cultivating a results-oriented peer-learning program in a corporate

### Reality

Cisco's internal peer learning network collapsed after eighteen months. Not from lack of engagement — the forums buzzed, the wikis grew, people showed up. It collapsed because nobody could answer a simple question: who decides what counts as learning? The facilitators pointed to the community. The community pointed to management. Management pointed to learning objectives that existed only in PowerPoint.

The workscape promised to be different. No rigid curricula, no top-down mandates — just professionals learning from each other in their natural habitat. But every workscape I've watched has the same obituary: death by infinite recursion. Someone asks "What should we learn?" The group asks "What do you think we should learn?" Round and round until the budget runs out.

Corporate learning ecosystems don't fail because they lack peer connection. They fail because they refuse to name what they're optimizing for — and peer learning without direction isn't learning, it's group therapy with better coffee.

> What if the workscape isn't a platform for learning but a map of everything the organization doesn't want to admit it doesn't know?

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that workscapes may function less as learning environments and more as organizational defense mechanisms — spaces where the appearance of peer learning masks the absence of genuine knowledge transfer. When Cisco's network imploded, what died wasn't peer connection but the fiction that learning happens automatically when you put smart people in a room together.

The distinction between formal and informal learning breaks down under pressure. What remains to be tested is whether workscapes can survive the honesty required to name their actual function — not cultivating results, but managing the anxiety that comes from not knowing what results to cultivate.
