"A lack of explicit hierarchy does not constitute an absence of power relations." "Some are more equal than others, even in a peeragogical setting."

The Handbook recognizes that power exists even in flat groups. Good. Then it doesn't say what to do about it.

**Why it matters:** Peeragogy's biggest blind spot is power. The theory presents flat structure as a solution. But flat structure doesn't eliminate power. It hides it. When power is hidden, it's harder to contest. The group pretends there's no hierarchy. But there is. The ones who speak more, decide more. The ones who've been there longer, influence more. The ones who produce more, control more.

The Handbook acknowledges the problem intellectually. Then doesn't propose tools to address it. Because addressing power would require naming it. And naming it would break the egalitarian narrative. It's easier to theorize power than to face it.

**What to do instead:** Name power. Explicitly. "Who decides when there's no consensus?" If the answer is "the group decides," that's not true. Someone decides. Always. Find who. Name it. The first step to managing power is admitting it exists. The second is deciding how to distribute it. The Handbook is stuck at step one.

> **Perturbator:** *"You say there's no hierarchy. But who decides the agenda? Who closes discussions? Who decides what's a priority? You don't have someone with a title. You have someone with influence. Influence is power. Invisible power is the worst kind. At least with a title, you know who to blame. Invisible power is impossible to confront."*
