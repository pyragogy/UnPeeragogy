### The Assumption

Power exists in flat groups but remains hidden and unaddressed, creating invisible dynamics that complicate accountability and decision-making through mechanisms like knowledge-hoarding and authority-attrition.

### Operational Reality

Three engineers at a fintech startup spent six months building "consensus-driven architecture decisions." They documented every choice, rotated meeting facilitation, and tracked participation metrics. The system worked — until the day their lead architect quietly migrated the entire codebase to a framework none of them had discussed. When confronted, she shrugged: "You were debating database schemas. I was solving the real problem."

Power does not announce itself with org charts and voting procedures. Not formal authority. Not documented processes. Not even explicit hierarchy. It flows through the gaps — who controls the narrative, who frames the questions, who decides what counts as a problem worth solving. The Wikipedia editor who writes the daily summary email shapes tomorrow's priorities more than the elected board members who meet monthly.

GitLab shows that documented governance boundaries between stakeholder groups can create structural limits on power. Wikipedia shows that visibility can institutionalize power imbalances rather than eliminate them. Debian's DPL bits newsletter demonstrates that curation itself is a form of power that transparency alone does not dissolve.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if naming the dragon does not kill it — sometimes it just teaches the dragon better PR — and the most dangerous power is the kind that insists it does not exist?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [GitLab Stewardship Handbook](https://about.gitlab.com/handbook/company/stewardship/) — Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects. Documented governance boundaries between stakeholder groups can create structural limits on power.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [Wikipedia Deletion Debates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Articles_for_deletion) — Consensus processes produce de facto hierarchies through participation inequality — those who show up consistently gain disproportionate influence. Visibility can institutionalize power imbalances rather than eliminate them.
- [Debian DPL Bits Newsletter](https://lists.debian.org/debian-devel-announce/) — Regular leadership summaries in peer-governed projects create agenda-setting power — what gets summarized is what gets attention. Curation itself is a form of power that transparency alone does not dissolve.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** The pattern works when visibility is coupled with explicit redistribution mechanisms — documented governance boundaries between different stakeholders reduce friction by creating structural limits on asymmetric power.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not assume that making power dynamics visible dissolves them automatically — transparent processes can institutionalize power imbalances; visibility without redistribution is performative transparency.

### Epistemic Synthesis

Power analysis fails when it focuses on visible structures instead of invisible rhythms. The sponsor who documents governance boundaries can reduce friction — but the documentation itself becomes a form of control, determining what can be contested and what stays off the agenda. Peer governance that produces leadership summaries creates the illusion of transparency while concentrating agenda-setting power in the hands of those who choose what deserves attention. The most effective power operates through curation — not blocking access, but shaping what seems worth accessing.
