### Claim

The node states that power exists in flat groups but remains hidden and unaddressed, creating invisible dynamics that complicate accountability and decision-making through mechanisms like knowledge-hoarding and authority-attrition.

### Reality

Three engineers at a fintech startup spent six months building "consensus-driven architecture decisions." They documented every choice, rotated meeting facilitation, and tracked participation metrics. The system worked — until the day their lead architect quietly migrated the entire codebase to a framework none of them had discussed. When confronted, she shrugged: "You were debating database schemas. I was solving the real problem."

Power doesn't announce itself with org charts and voting procedures. Not formal authority. Not documented processes. Not even explicit hierarchy. It flows through the gaps — who controls the narrative, who frames the questions, who decides what counts as a problem worth solving.

The Wikipedia editor who writes the daily summary email shapes tomorrow's priorities more than the elected board members who meet monthly.

> What if the most dangerous power is the kind that insists it doesn't exist?

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that power analysis fails when it focuses on visible structures instead of invisible rhythms. The corporate sponsor who documents governance boundaries may reduce friction — but documentation itself becomes a form of control, determining what can be contested and what remains beyond debate. 

The peer governance that produces leadership summaries creates the illusion of transparency while concentrating agenda-setting power in the hands of those who choose what deserves attention. The most effective power operates through curation — not by blocking access, but by shaping what seems worth accessing.

What remains to be tested is whether recognizing these patterns changes them, or simply makes their operation more sophisticated.
