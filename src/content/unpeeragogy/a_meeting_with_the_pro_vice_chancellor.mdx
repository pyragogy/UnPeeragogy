### Claim

Author: Laura Ritchie

### Reality

Laura Ritchie walks into the Pro Vice Chancellor's office with printouts and enthusiasm. She's done this before — the careful explanation, the patient demonstration, the hope that institutional weight might finally recognize what's already working in her classroom. The Dean listens politely, nods at appropriate intervals, asks about metrics and scalability.

Not funding. Not infrastructure. Not recognition of existing practice.

The conversation shifts to compliance frameworks and quality assurance. How do you measure peer learning outcomes? Where's the accountability structure? What happens when students teach each other incorrectly? Laura finds herself defending something that doesn't need defense — it works, her students engage, learning happens. But institutional language has no vocabulary for useful friction, for the productive struggle of figuring things out together.

The meeting ends with promises to "explore possibilities" and "circle back." Laura leaves with the same printouts she brought in.

> What if the Dean's confusion isn't about peeragogy — but about recognizing learning that doesn't fit institutional measurement?

### Synthesis

These encounters expose the gap between lived practice and institutional recognition. Laura's students already experience peer learning — they form study groups, explain concepts to each other, build knowledge collectively. The Dean's office operates in a different reality, one where learning must be packaged, assessed, and scaled according to existing frameworks.

The tension isn't pedagogical but administrative. Institutions require clear hierarchies for liability, defined outcomes for accreditation, measurable results for funding. Peer learning resists these containers — it's messy, emergent, hard to predict. What works in Laura's classroom may not translate to the Dean's spreadsheet, not because it's ineffective, but because it operates outside institutional grammar.

The question remains whether peeragogy needs institutional validation to spread — or whether seeking that validation fundamentally changes what it is.
