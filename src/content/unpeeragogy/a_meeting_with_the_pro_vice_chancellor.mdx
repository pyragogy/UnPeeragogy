### The Assumption

Peer learning should be self-evident. If you need to argue that peer learning works to an institutional authority, you have already lost sight of the principle. Peeragogy resists institutional mediation because its value is immediately visible in practice, not in a compliance framework.

### Operational Reality

Laura Ritchie — a real participant documented in the handbook — walked into a Pro Vice Chancellor's office with printouts and enthusiasm. She explains peer learning, shows what her students are doing, makes the case for institutional support. The conversation shifts: metrics, accountability structures, quality assurance protocols. The institutional language has no vocabulary for productive friction, for the struggle of figuring things out together that is the actual mechanism of peer learning.

The meeting ends with promises to "explore possibilities." Laura leaves with the same printouts she brought in.

The handbook presents this as a recognition problem: the institution does not see what peer learning offers. But the more instructive reading is that peer learning as a practice cannot be argued into institutional form. It must be housed, and housing requires institutional translation — defined roles, explicit governance boundaries, recognizable credentialing pathways. P2PU succeeded where it built institutional partnerships with defined facilitator roles. Fedora succeeded where elected steering committees with transparent records distributed governance at scale. Neither succeeded through the persuasive power of the practice alone.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if the Dean's confusion isn't about peeragogy — but about recognizing learning that doesn't fit institutional measurement?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [P2PU Learning Circles](https://www.p2pu.org/en/learning-circles/) — Peer learning models with defined facilitator roles and institutional partnerships are more sustainable than purely peer-driven learning. The container was designed, not discovered.
- [Fedora Engineering Steering Committee](https://docs.fedoraproject.org/en-US/fesco/) — Elected technical steering committees with defined scope and transparent records provide sustainable peer governance at scale. Institutional design preceded institutional recognition.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [Drupal Governance](https://www.drupal.org/governance) — Governance transition from BDFL to community model required years of structural work, not organic emergence. The assumption that peer governance emerges naturally contradicts Drupal's documented experience.
- [P2PU Governance](https://www.p2pu.org/en/about/) — Peer learning communities face credentialing and quality assurance crises that require hierarchy or external validation frameworks — exactly what the theory claims they do not need.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Develop explicit governance structures and institutional translation mechanisms before seeking institutional recognition — defined roles and boundaries create the vocabulary the institution needs to say yes.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not approach institutional authorities with the claim that peer learning "speaks for itself" — the Dean has no vocabulary for productive friction, and the assumption of self-evidence is not a funding strategy.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The assumption that peer learning's validity is immediately visible in practice is contradicted by every successful case in the archive. P2PU and Fedora required explicit institutional design, defined roles, and external validation frameworks. The tension is not pedagogical but administrative: the assumption that "it should be obvious" has never secured a budget line. The documented meeting with Laura Ritchie is not a failure of institutional vision but a case study in what happens when a practice built on productive friction meets an institution built on measurable compliance.
