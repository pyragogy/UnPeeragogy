### Claim

You might wonder why we're doing this project -- what we hope to get out

### Reality

The 5PH1NX project died three months after their community manager started writing weekly summaries. Not because the summaries were bad — they were excellent. Comprehensive updates on code commits, user feedback, roadmap progress. The problem was simpler: the summaries became the project.

People stopped reading the actual discussions. They waited for Sarah's digest instead. When Sarah got promoted and stopped writing them, the community had forgotten how to pay attention to itself. The infrastructure was still there — forums, chat channels, issue trackers. But the rhythm was broken. Without someone else's eyes organizing their chaos into neat paragraphs, they couldn't see their own work.

This wasn't laziness. It was structural blindness — the moment you hand over the work of seeing to someone else, you lose the muscle for it.

> What if every summary is teaching people to stop reading the original?

### Synthesis

The evidence suggests that summaries function as governance tools disguised as information services. When done by insiders, they create agenda-setting power — what gets summarized becomes what matters. When done by outsiders, they risk replacing direct engagement with mediated understanding.

The tension here may not be between good summaries and bad ones, but between the convenience of delegation and the necessity of firsthand attention. Communities that rely on summaries to know themselves might be trading immediate clarity for long-term capacity to self-organize. What remains untested is whether this trade-off can be made consciously — whether communities can use summaries as training wheels rather than permanent prosthetics.
