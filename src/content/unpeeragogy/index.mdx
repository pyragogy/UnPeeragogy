### Claim

*News:*

### Reality

The Peeragogy Handbook opens with a welcome mat and a Google form. Two links, one promise, zero indication of what actually works inside. This is how most peer learning projects present themselves — as if the invitation itself were the method.

Not a curriculum. Not a tested sequence. Not even a warning about what breaks.

The handbook treats its own index like neutral territory. Each pattern gets equal billing, same formatting, democratic placement. But three years of watching groups attempt peeragogy reveals the opposite: some patterns kill projects, others save them, most do nothing at all. The index refuses to admit this.

> What if an index that concealed failure rates was actually protecting something more fragile than success?

### Evidence That Supports

**about.gitlab.com** -- Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.


### Evidence That Complicates

**drupal.org** -- Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model requires years of structural work, not organic emergence.

**blog.makerdao.com** -- DAO governance mechanisms designed for normal operations can fail catastrophically under stress, requiring emergency governance that existing processes don't cover.

### Synthesis

The evidence suggests that governance documentation works precisely because it names what won't be tolerated — not what will be celebrated. Explicit boundaries reduce friction by making power visible, not by making it disappear. The DAO failures under stress reveal the same pattern: emergency governance succeeds when it abandons the pretense of consensus, not when it perfects it.

What remains untested is whether peer learning can survive its own honesty. An index that ranked patterns by survival rate, that marked the dead ones, that admitted which combinations reliably fail — this might serve learners better than the current welcome mat. But it would also expose how much we don't know about what we're asking people to attempt.
