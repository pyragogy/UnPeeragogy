### The Assumption

Welcoming newcomers requires balancing energy investment in onboarding against the disruption to existing group dynamics, with veterans potentially resenting the attention given to newcomers.

### Illustrative Scenario — Not Empirical Evidence

The following vignette is a **synthetic scenario** used to make the mechanism legible. It is not a field report and is not counted as empirical evidence.

Maria joins a neighborhood mutual aid network after six months of watching from the sidewalk. She shows up to the monthly meeting with a notebook full of ideas about food distribution efficiency. The organizers smile and nod while she talks about route optimization and inventory tracking. Three weeks later, she stops coming to meetings.

Not because they were mean to her. Not because they shut down her suggestions. Not because she felt unwelcome — everyone was genuinely kind, patient, encouraging even. She left because being nice to newcomers sidesteps the harder question: what happens when the newcomer's fresh perspective conflicts with how things actually get done?

In the scenario, the mutual aid group has spent two years building relationships with corner store owners who let them use back rooms for free. Maria's efficiency improvements would require renting warehouse space and buying software. Her ideas are not necessarily wrong — they are incompatible with the social infrastructure that makes the work possible in the first place.

The scenario generates a hypothesis, not a finding: **newcomer friction can expose dependencies that established members no longer perceive as decisions.**

### Documentary Evidence

The documentary cases below do not all support the hypothesis with the same force. That difference is explicit.

**Direct structural support:**
- [OpenStreetMap Data Working Group](https://wiki.osmfoundation.org/wiki/Data_Working_Group) — OpenStreetMap documents a formal body for disputes that exceed ordinary community resolution. This supports the narrower claim that peer-production systems may create explicit escalation authority when informal mechanisms are insufficient. It does **not** establish that newcomer-veteran disputes are the cause of that design.

**Contextual cases — not causal confirmation:**
- [WebAssembly Community Group](https://www.w3.org/community/webassembly/) — The W3C page documents a Community Group with named chairs and participation structures. It is relevant as governance context, but the source does not demonstrate that lightweight governance makes newcomer-veteran conflict irresolvable.
- [IETF](https://www.ietf.org/about/) — The IETF documents an institutional setting in which rough consensus coexists with formal organisational and process structures. This complicates any simple equation of "peer governance" with structurelessness, but the general About page does not by itself prove that escalation structures cause consensus to succeed.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if being nice to newcomers does not solve the problem of what happens when they disagree with you — and the newcomer's disruption is not a bug to be managed, but useful friction that exposes what the system actually depends on?"*

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Make hidden dependencies and decision rights visible during onboarding. If a group has escalation or dispute-resolution mechanisms, name them explicitly rather than allowing newcomers to infer that every decision is equally open.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not treat courtesy, openness of participation, or an invitation to speak as evidence that a newcomer has equal access to the dependencies that actually constrain decisions.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The current evidence supports a narrower claim than the original version of this entry made. Open peer-production systems can combine participatory rhetoric with explicit authority structures; those structures may become visible precisely where ordinary community mechanisms reach their limits. What remains insufficiently evidenced here is the stronger causal claim that newcomer-veteran disagreement specifically produces those limits.

The synthetic Maria scenario therefore remains useful as a **hypothesis generator**, not as proof. A field report describing a real newcomer entering an established peer group — including what was negotiable, what was not, and how disagreement was resolved — would materially strengthen or challenge this interpretation.

Kindness is not governance. But neither does the existence of governance prove why a conflict occurred.

### What Would Change This Interpretation?

- A field report in which newcomer disagreement is resolved sustainably without any explicit escalation mechanism would complicate the current boundary.
- Evidence showing that the cited formal bodies were created for reasons unrelated to limits of informal resolution would narrow the structural analogy.
- Multiple incidents showing that newcomers can renegotiate hidden dependencies without formal authority would weaken the hypothesis that visible decision rights are necessary.
