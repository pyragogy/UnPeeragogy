### Claim

This pattern can help project participants be aware of the issues faced

### Reality

Maria joins the neighborhood mutual aid network after six months of watching from the sidewalk. She shows up to the monthly meeting with a notebook full of ideas about food distribution efficiency. The organizers smile and nod while she talks about route optimization and inventory tracking. Three weeks later, she stops coming to meetings.

Not because they were mean to her. Not because they shut down her suggestions. Not because she felt unwelcome — everyone was genuinely kind, patient, encouraging even. She left because being nice to newcomers sidesteps the harder question: what happens when the newcomer's fresh perspective conflicts with how things actually get done?

The mutual aid group had spent two years building relationships with corner store owners who let them use back rooms for free. Maria's efficiency improvements would have required renting warehouse space and buying software. Her ideas weren't wrong — they were incompatible with the social infrastructure that made the work possible in the first place.

> What if the newcomer's disruption isn't a bug to be managed, but useful friction that exposes what the system actually depends on?

### Evidence That Supports

### Synthesis

The evidence suggests that welcoming newcomers requires more than beginner's mind cultivation — it demands honest reckoning with existing power structures. Formal dispute resolution works precisely because it makes authority visible rather than hiding it behind consensus theater. When Maria's suggestions got absorbed into polite listening sessions, the real decision-making process stayed invisible to her.

The tension isn't between old and new perspectives. It's between the mythology of open participation and the reality that all systems have non-negotiable dependencies — relationships, resources, unspoken agreements that newcomers can't see. What remains untested is whether groups can name these constraints honestly enough that newcomers can choose their battles rather than waste energy fighting shadows.
