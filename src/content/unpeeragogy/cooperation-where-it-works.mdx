### Claim

The node states that cooperation emerges when work is modular (allowing independent progress), when there's shared vision despite tactical disagreements, and when asymmetry in contributions is accepted rather than resented.

### Reality

The Kubernetes project ships releases every three months. Over 3,000 contributors across dozens of companies somehow coordinate changes to a codebase that runs half the internet's workloads. No central authority decides what gets merged — yet the system holds together. Not through consensus meetings. Not through democratic voting. Not through benevolent dictatorship.

Through boundaries that everyone can see.

Each Special Interest Group owns specific modules. The steering committee handles conflicts between SIGs, not technical decisions within them. Corporate sponsors fund development but cannot override community governance. When Red Hat or Google wants a feature, they contribute code through the same review process as weekend hobbyists. The asymmetry is visible — some contributors write more code, others review more pull requests, others focus on documentation. But the governance boundaries prevent any single actor from capturing the whole system.

This is cooperation that has survived corporate acquisitions, leadership changes, and explosive growth from dozens to thousands of contributors.

> What if the pattern isn't about eliminating hierarchy, but about making power visible enough to resist?

### Evidence That Supports

**about.gitlab.com** -- Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.

**blender.org** -- Foundation governance with a board, CTO, and module owners can sustain complex peer production for decades.


### Evidence That Complicates

**moodle.org** -- Successful educational open-source projects require explicit governance transitions from founder-led to community-led models.

**drupal.org** -- Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model requires years of structural work, not organic emergence.

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that cooperation requires explicit friction, not its elimination. The boundaries between modules, between corporate and community interests, between different types of contribution — these create useful resistance that prevents any single force from overwhelming the system. Kubernetes works because you cannot simply buy your way to control. You must earn influence through sustained contribution within visible constraints.

What remains to be tested is whether this governance model can transfer beyond software projects. The modularity is technical, but the boundaries are social — and those social boundaries may require the specific culture of open-source development to hold. The cooperation depends not just on shared vision, but on shared resistance to capture.
