### The Assumption

Cooperation emerges when work is modular (allowing independent progress), when there is shared vision despite tactical disagreements, and when asymmetry in contributions is accepted rather than resented. These conditions produce sustainable collaboration without explicit governance overhead.

### Operational Reality

The Kubernetes project ships releases every three months. Over 3,000 contributors across dozens of companies coordinate changes to a codebase that runs half the internet's workloads. No central authority decides what gets merged — yet the system holds together. Not through consensus meetings. Not through democratic voting. Not through benevolent dictatorship. Through boundaries that everyone can see.

Each Special Interest Group owns specific modules. The steering committee handles conflicts between SIGs, not technical decisions within them. Corporate sponsors fund development but cannot override community governance. When Red Hat or Google wants a feature, they contribute code through the same review process as weekend hobbyists. The asymmetry is visible — some contributors write more code, others review more pull requests, others focus on documentation. But the governance boundaries prevent any single actor from capturing the whole system.

GitLab shows that modularity without boundaries is just fragmentation. Blender demonstrates that explicit governance — not just modular work — enables long-term cooperation. Moodle shows that the assumption "modularity enables cooperation" misses the governance transition problem entirely. Drupal confirms that even modular projects hit coordination crises when informal structures meet scaling pressure.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if modularity gets you started, but governance keeps you alive — and the pattern is not about eliminating hierarchy, but about making power visible enough to resist?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [GitLab Stewardship Handbook](https://about.gitlab.com/handbook/company/stewardship/) — Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects. Modularity without boundaries is just fragmentation.
- [Blender Foundation Governance](https://www.blender.org/about/foundation/) — Foundation governance with a board, CTO, and module owners can sustain complex peer production for decades. Explicit governance — not just modular work — enables long-term cooperation.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [Moodle Community Governance](https://moodle.org/dev/Governance) — Successful educational open-source projects require explicit governance transitions from founder-led to community-led models. The assumption "modularity enables cooperation" misses the governance transition problem entirely.
- [Drupal Governance](https://www.drupal.org/governance) — Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model required years of structural work, not organic emergence. Even modular projects hit coordination crises when informal structures meet scaling pressure.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Apply cooperative modular patterns only when combined with explicit governance documentation from the start — GitLab and Blender demonstrate that clear boundaries and defined decision-making processes make modular cooperation sustainable.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not apply modular cooperation to scaling projects without planning governance transitions — Moodle and Drupal show that even successful modular projects hit coordination crises when informal structures meet scaling pressure.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The evidence challenges the assumption that modularity + shared vision + accepted asymmetry are sufficient conditions. Kubernetes and Blender work because their governance structures — not just work modularity — prevent capture by single actors. Moodle and Drupal show that cooperation crises are not caused by lack of modularity but by invisible governance that does not scale. Modularity gets you started. Governance keeps you alive.
