### Claim

Our solution builds on these methods: Patterns, Next Steps, PARs, and CLAs.

### Reality

The Debian project ships a new stable release every two years. Twenty thousand packages, tested across eleven architectures, with zero corporate oversight. Their secret weapon isn't consensus — it's the Technical Committee, seven developers who make binding decisions when the community deadlocks. Democracy with an escape hatch.

Not peer-driven utopia. Not corporate hierarchy. Not the false choice between chaos and control.

Most peer learning initiatives collapse at the first hard decision. Someone has to say no to a beloved contributor. Someone has to cut a feature that half the group wants. Someone has to choose between two equally passionate factions. The facilitator role sounds collaborative until it requires actual authority — then the peers discover they never agreed on who gets to decide what.

The honest projects document this upfront. Kubernetes has a steering committee. Python has Guido's successor council. Even Wikipedia has arbitrators who can ban editors permanently. The governance isn't hidden behind collaborative language — it's explicit, bounded, and earned through demonstrated judgment.

> What if the most peer-driven communities are actually the ones honest enough to name their hierarchies?

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**ietf.org** -- Rough consensus governance only works when backed by hierarchical escalation paths; pure consensus without appeals fails.

**en.wikipedia.org** -- The Wikipedia Arbitration Committee (ArbCom) case in 2021 highlighted conflicts in community governance, where differing views on content moderation led to heated debates. The theoretical pattern posits that shared governance leads to consensus, yet this case illustrates how diverse opinions can complicate decision-making processes, resulting in protracted disputes.


### Synthesis

These cases suggest that sustainable peer learning may require what we might call "useful friction" — explicit boundaries that prevent the community from having to renegotiate fundamental questions every time conflict emerges. The most resilient projects seem to distinguish between areas of genuine collaboration and areas where someone simply has to decide.

The tension isn't between peer-driven and hierarchical models. It's between communities that acknowledge their decision-making reality and those that pretend every choice can be consensus-driven. What remains to be tested is whether peer learning initiatives can survive their own honesty about power — or whether the collaborative ideal depends on never naming who actually holds it.
