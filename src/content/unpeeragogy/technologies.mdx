## Summary

Tools multiply faster than governance structures. The theory says peer selection of technology creates collective ownership — but practice shows it creates collective paralysis when authority boundaries are unclear.

## Euristiche Operative

### Positive Rule

> This pattern works when governance boundaries are explicit and peer input is bounded by clear authority structures. GitLab's stewardship handbook shows that explicit boundaries between corporate sponsors and community reduce friction while maintaining peer involvement in technology decisions.

### Negative Rule

> Do not apply this pattern when technology choices create externalities for non-participants or when decision authority is completely diffuse. Mastodon's defederation mechanics show that peer governance can disenfranchise affected users, and diffuse authority creates decision paralysis.

## Sintesi Obliqua

The pattern claims peer technology selection builds ownership through consensus. Partial verification — it works when governance boundaries are explicit, like GitLab's stewardship handbook separating corporate from community decisions. But Mastodon's defederation mechanics show how peer governance creates externalities for users with no voting rights, and Linux's diffuse review authority demonstrates that collective ownership can mean nobody owns the decision. The real mechanism isn't consensus — it's bounded authority with clear escalation paths. Democracy assumes all stakeholders get a vote.
