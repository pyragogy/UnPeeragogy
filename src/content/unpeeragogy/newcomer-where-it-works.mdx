### Claim

The node presents Charlotte's integration story as a pattern where newcomers become active contributors through weekly meetings and welcoming culture, with emergent roles developing naturally in peer learning environments.

### Reality

Charlotte joined through weekly meetings and stayed for the welcoming culture. The handbook celebrates this as pattern success — newcomer becomes contributor through gentle integration. I've watched this story play out dozens of times in maker spaces, open source projects, community workshops.

Most Charlottes leave after three meetings.

Not because the meetings aren't nice. Not because people aren't welcoming. Not because the culture fails to embrace them. The meetings are lovely, the people genuine, the culture exactly as advertised. But Charlotte sits through week four wondering what she's supposed to do next, and week five never comes. The pattern works perfectly — right up until it doesn't.

The communities that retain newcomers don't just have welcoming cultures. They have friction that teaches. Clear contribution paths that pressure-test interest against capability. Ways to fail small before failing large.

> What if the warmest welcome is a concrete task you can complete badly?

**ietf.org** -- Rough consensus governance only works when backed by hierarchical escalation paths; pure consensus without appeals fails.

**w3.org** -- Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes for peer participation.

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that integration happens through useful work, not comfortable meetings. Charlotte's story works when the weekly gatherings create visible paths to contribution — not just social connection, but structured ways to test skills against real problems.

The peer assessment systems that survive depend on this same principle. Transparent criteria matter less than reviewable work. Independent reviewers help, but what holds the system together is that newcomers can see exactly how their contributions get evaluated — and improve them.

The distinction may be between communities that optimize for belonging versus communities that optimize for becoming. One feels better. The other teaches more.
