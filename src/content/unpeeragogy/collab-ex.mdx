### Claim

Authors: Peter Taylor and Teryl Cartwright

### Reality

Peter Taylor sits in a room with twelve strangers, notebooks open, ready to "shape their own directions of inquiry." The facilitator explains the process — small groups, delimited time periods, collaborative investigation. What happens next isn't discovery. It's negotiation disguised as exploration.

I've watched this pattern repeat in makerspaces, research collectives, peer learning circles. The group fractures within hours. Not over what to explore — over who gets to decide what counts as exploration. Someone always emerges as the unofficial coordinator. Someone else resists that emergence. The "collaborative" part becomes a fight about collaboration itself.

The handbook promises investigation and teaching in equal measure. But teaching requires authority someone must grant or claim. Investigation demands direction someone must choose or refuse.

> What if the real exploration happens in the spaces between official collaboration — in the friction that formal processes can't capture?

### Evidence That Supports

**community.kde.org** -- Elected peer governance councils with defined authority and transparent records provide stable long-term community governance.

**wiki.osmfoundation.org** -- Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot.


### Evidence That Complicates

**wiki.debian.org** -- Technical decisions in peer communities can cause permanent splits when decision-making authority is contested.


### Evidence That Contradicts

*No case in the current corpus directly contradicts the pattern.*

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**blog.makerdao.com** -- DAO governance mechanisms designed for normal operations can fail catastrophically under stress, requiring emergency governance that existing processes don't cover.


### Synthesis

What if the real exploration happens in the spaces between official collaboration — in the friction that formal processes can't capture?


These cases suggest something the theory doesn't account for — exploration and collaboration may be fundamentally at odds. True exploration requires the freedom to abandon direction, change course, follow tangents that lead nowhere. Collaboration requires enough shared commitment to stay together through that uncertainty.

The evidence from peer governance points to a deeper tension. Formal structures can resolve disputes, but they can't resolve the question of whether resolution itself serves exploration. When technical communities split permanently over contested authority, they may be choosing exploration over collaboration — choosing the unknown over the negotiated.

What remains untested is whether the most honest collaborative exploration might be the kind that admits it cannot stay collaborative — and doesn't try to.
