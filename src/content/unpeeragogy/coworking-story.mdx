### Reality

Julian walks into the housing association boardroom with a folder of peeragogical principles and finds twelve people who need to cut £2.3 million from next year's budget. The government changed the benefits formula. Construction loans dried up. Three board members represent tenant groups who will lose services.

Not collaboration theory. Not consensus building. Not dissolved hierarchies.

The work happens in the space between what sounds good in principle and what survives contact with a spreadsheet. Julian's approach — research factors, draw themes together, find strategic options — assumes the luxury of exploration. But when you're managing 4,000 housing units and the funding model just collapsed, exploration feels like procrastination.

### Synthesis

The evidence suggests something uncomfortable about peer learning in institutional settings. Explicit governance boundaries help — knowing who decides what reduces the friction between idealistic process and necessary authority. But this points toward a deeper tension that peeragogy hasn't fully addressed.

The housing association board exists because someone has to sign the contracts and someone has to get fired when things go wrong. Beautiful stories about dissolved hierarchies don't change the fact that liability flows upward, not laterally. What remains to be tested is whether peer learning can coexist with the weight of institutional responsibility — or whether it only works in spaces where the stakes stay low.
