### Reality

The P2PU course organizers discovered something most peer learning advocates miss. Their best experiences happened when they stopped trying to organize and started pointing instead. Not at curriculum. Not at outcomes. At the moments when someone else stepped forward.

You can document governance boundaries all you want — the real landmarks emerge when people test what actually holds under pressure. Complex processes designed to include everyone end up serving the people who already know how to navigate complexity. Lighter models trade one kind of failure for another. The friction doesn't disappear. It just moves.

What the organizers learned wasn't theory. It was recognition — that the thing you're pointing at might not be the thing people need to see.

> What if the landmarks you're creating are exactly what's keeping people from finding their own way?

### Synthesis

These cases suggest something uncomfortable about peer learning infrastructure. The more explicit we make the boundaries, the more we entrench the people who already understand boundary-making. The governance that reduces friction for insiders may generate different friction for everyone else.

What remains to be tested is whether landmarks can serve navigation without becoming barriers — whether the thing that helps you point the way can resist becoming the thing that blocks it.
