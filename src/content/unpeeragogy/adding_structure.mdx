### Reality

The library example from the handbook sounds clean until you try to build one. I spent three months helping a neighborhood group turn an abandoned storefront into a community learning space. We had grand ideas about organic structure — people would naturally create what they needed, protocols would emerge from use, the space would self-organize around genuine learning.

Six weeks in, nothing worked. Books disappeared. Meeting rooms stayed booked by the same three people. New members couldn't find anything. The quiet study area became a social club for teenagers who scared off older learners. Not because people were malicious — because useful friction requires intentional design.

The turning point came when we installed the most boring possible system: a clipboard signup sheet, numbered shelves, and a volunteer schedule. Suddenly the space held. People could count on finding what they expected. The structure didn't constrain creativity — it freed people from having to solve the same coordination problems over and over.

Structure earns trust by being predictable when everything else shifts.

> What if the structures we resist most are exactly the ones that would let us take bigger creative risks?

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that structure functions as a trust technology, not a creativity constraint. The neighborhood learning space needed rules precisely because it served strangers — people with no shared history to fall back on when coordination failed. The clipboard and numbered shelves weren't bureaucracy; they were the minimum viable infrastructure for strangers to collaborate without constant negotiation.

The pattern may be that peer learning scales through deliberate structure, not despite it. Wikipedia's editing guidelines, Linux's patch review process, even the simple ritual of taking turns speaking — these aren't creativity killers but creativity enablers. They handle the boring coordination work so human attention can focus on the interesting problems.

What remains to be tested is whether we can design structures that degrade gracefully — systems that provide scaffolding for newcomers while staying invisible to experienced users who no longer need the guardrails.
