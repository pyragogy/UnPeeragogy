### Claim

Peeragogy identifies magical thinking as the enemy of real learning. The theory says that when a group faces a difficult problem, it defaults to shortcuts — imagining that solutions will emerge without work, that consensus will form without conflict, that someone else will handle the hard part. Magical thinking robs a context of its process, its motion, its learning potential. The solution: replace fantasy with explicit governance.

### Reality

Groups default to magical thinking because formal coordination has already failed them.

It is not a cognitive shortcut. It is a survival mechanism. When the group has no structure to resolve disagreements, no process for making decisions, no authority that can unblock — the members fall back on fantasy because fantasy is less painful than admitting the group is not equipped to do what it claims to want.

Peeragogy prescribes explicit governance as the cure. And it is right — when the governance exists and works. GitLab's stewardship handbook and OpenStreetMap's Data Working Group prove it: formal boundaries and clear decision-making channels replace magical thinking with functional coordination.

But the theory misses the harder case. Drupal's transition from BDFL to community governance took years. RISC-V's foundation moved to Switzerland without asking the community. These are not failures of magical thinking. They are cases where the magic was load-bearing — and replacing it broke things that were working.

> **Perturbatore:** *"You call it magical thinking. They call it hope. And hope is what keeps a group together when the structure is not ready. Replace the magic too early, and you do not get governance. You get collapse. The question is not whether the magic is real. The question is whether you have something better to put in its place."*

### Positive Examples

**GitLab stewardship handbook** — Explicit boundaries between corporate sponsor and community. When the governance is clear, nobody needs magical thinking. Everyone knows where money ends and autonomy begins.

**OpenStreetMap Data Working Group** — A formal dispute resolution body with binding authority. When conflict arises, the DWG decides. Fantasy is not required because the process exists.

### Negative Examples

**Drupal governance transition** — Moving from BDFL to community model required years of deliberate structural work. The informal coordination was not magical thinking — it was functioning. Premature formalization would have destroyed working relationships before the new structure was ready.

**RISC-V foundation move to Switzerland** — The foundation changed legal domicile and IP jurisdiction without community consent. The decision was binding, legal, and exposed the limits of peer governance in incorporated projects. Magic was not the problem. Incorporation was.

### Synthesis

Do not treat magical thinking as the disease. Treat it as a symptom of missing infrastructure.

If a group is fantasizing that things will work out, ask: what structure is missing? Fix the structure first. The fantasy will dissolve on its own.

But do not rip out functioning informal coordination to replace it with premature formal governance. Drupal's transition teaches that the informal is sometimes load-bearing. Replace it only when the formal alternative is ready to carry the weight.

The goal is not to eliminate magical thinking. The goal is to make it unnecessary.
