### Claim

The Peeragogy Handbook identifies magical thinking as the enemy of real learning. When a group faces a difficult problem, it defaults to shortcuts — imagining that solutions will emerge without work, that consensus will form without conflict, that someone else will handle the hard part. The theory argues that magical thinking robs a context of its process and motion. The proposed solution: replace fantasy with explicit governance.

### Reality

Groups default to magical thinking when formal coordination has already failed them.

It is not a cognitive shortcut. It is a survival mechanism. When the group has no structure to resolve disagreements, no process for making decisions, no authority to unblock — members fall back on fantasy because fantasy is less painful than admitting the group is not equipped.

Peeragogy prescribes explicit governance as the cure. And it is right — when the governance exists and works. GitLab's stewardship handbook and OpenStreetMap's Data Working Group prove that formal boundaries can replace magical thinking with functional coordination.

But the theory misses the harder case. Drupal's transition from BDFL to community governance took years. RISC-V's foundation moved to Switzerland without asking the community. These are not failures of magical thinking. They are cases where the informal coordination was load-bearing — and replacing it risked breaking things that were working.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if magical thinking is not the disease but the symptom — and the disease is the absence of functional governance? A group that fantasizes about a solution may be a group that has already lost trust in its own processes. The fantasy is not the problem. The lack of alternatives is."*

### Evidence That Supports

**GitLab stewardship handbook** — Explicit boundaries between corporate sponsor and community. When governance is clear, nobody needs magical thinking. Everyone knows where money ends and autonomy begins.

**OpenStreetMap Data Working Group** — A formal dispute resolution body with binding authority. When conflict arises, the DWG decides. Fantasy is not required because the process exists.

### Evidence That Complicates

**Drupal governance transition** — Moving from BDFL to community governance required years of deliberate structural work. The informal coordination was not magical thinking — it was functioning. Premature formalization would have destroyed working relationships before the new structure was ready.

**RISC-V foundation move to Switzerland** — The foundation changed legal domicile and IP jurisdiction without community consent. The decision was binding, legal, and exposed the limits of peer governance in incorporated projects. Magical thinking was not the problem. Incorporation law was.

### Evidence That Contradicts

*No case in the current corpus directly contradicts the pattern.*

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**Wikipedia Arbitration Committee** — Created after years of failed informal dispute resolution. It works. But it also shows that the transition from informal to formal governance is reactive, painful, and takes years. Whether proactive formalization would have been better — or would have destroyed trust prematurely — is not clear.

### Synthesis

What these cases suggest: do not treat magical thinking as the disease. Treat it as a symptom of missing infrastructure.

If a group is fantasizing that things will work out, ask: what structure is missing? Fix the structure first. The fantasy may dissolve on its own.

But do not rip out functioning informal coordination to replace it with premature formal governance. Drupal's transition teaches that the informal is sometimes load-bearing. Replace it only when the formal alternative is ready to carry the weight.

The goal is not to eliminate magical thinking. The goal is to make it unnecessary. Whether that is possible in all contexts — or only in groups small enough to sustain explicit governance — remains to be tested.
