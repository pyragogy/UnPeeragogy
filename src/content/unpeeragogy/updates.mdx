### Reality

The standup runs fifteen minutes. Same faces, same cadence, same careful selection of what gets said and what doesn't. Sarah mentions the API refactor but not the three-hour debugging session that revealed the real problem. Marcus updates on user research but skips the part where two participants quit mid-interview. The scrum master takes notes.

This isn't transparency — it's curation. Every update carries an implicit editorial decision about what deserves attention and what can wait. Not malicious. Not even conscious most of the time. But the pattern holds: regular communication creates regular power over the narrative.

The governance handbook says updates build trust through visibility. The lived reality is different. Updates become a contest for mindshare, where the most articulate voices shape what the group sees as urgent. The quiet work — the debugging, the user research failures, the small discoveries that don't fit the sprint vocabulary — gets pushed to the margins.

What looks like peer governance often runs on the unstated authority of who speaks first and who speaks best.

### Evidence That Complicates

**lists.debian.org** -- Regular leadership summaries in peer-governed projects create agenda-setting power — what gets summarized is what gets attention.

**w3.org** -- Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes for peer participation.

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that communication frequency may matter less than communication authority. Regular updates can democratize information or concentrate editorial power — the format alone doesn't determine which happens. What remains to be tested is whether peer governance can resist the gravitational pull of articulate voices without losing the benefits of regular rhythm.

The tension between speed and inclusion may not resolve through better process design. Lighter governance trades one set of failure modes for another — not necessarily better ones, just different. The friction between who speaks and who gets heard doesn't disappear when you flatten the hierarchy.
