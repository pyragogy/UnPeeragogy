The "Problem" page in the Handbook is one of the shortest. It says: "here we present some brief introductory texts that might feed into a revised introduction." It's a evasive definition of what a "problem" is in peer learning: an excuse to rewrite the introduction.

**Why it matters:** Defining the problem is the most elegant way not to solve it. As long as you're discussing "what the real problem is," you're doing nothing to address it. The group settles for an increasingly sophisticated definition of the problem — and mistakes diagnostic sophistication for progress.

The theory invites you to "frame the problem." Practice knows that framing is the last step before doing nothing. A well-framed problem demands a specific solution. A specific solution demands work. So you re-frame the problem. Add nuance. Widen the context. The problem becomes so complex that no one knows where to start. Great. The group feels smart and does nothing.

**What to do instead:** Define the problem in ten minutes. Then solve it. If you can't define it in ten minutes, you don't have a problem — you have an excuse not to work. A problem is whatever prevents you from moving forward. Everything else is conversation.

> **Perturbator:** *"Your problem isn't that you don't understand the problem. Your problem is that you spent three meetings defining it. Define it in ten minutes. Then solve it. If you can't define it in ten minutes, you don't have a problem — you have a reason to avoid working."*

**Reference:** Peeragogy Handbook, "Problem" section.
