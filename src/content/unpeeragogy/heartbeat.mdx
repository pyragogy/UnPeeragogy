### Reality

A weekly standup call dies after six weeks. The Slack channel goes quiet. Three people still care about the project, but nobody knows who they are. This happens to every distributed team that mistakes communication for connection — they schedule heartbeats without checking if anyone has a pulse.

Not ritual. Not regular check-ins. Not another meeting where people report what they did yesterday. The heartbeat that actually works is the one you feel when something breaks. When the server goes down and two people jump on a call at midnight. When someone posts a half-formed idea and three others start building on it immediately. When the work itself generates the rhythm.

Most teams engineer their heartbeat backwards. They create the meeting first, then hope motivation follows. But motivation doesn't respond to calendar invites.

> > What if the strongest heartbeat is the one that skips a beat — the irregular pulse of people who only connect when they actually need each other?"*

### Evidence That Supports

**wiki.osmfoundation.org** -- Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot.

**docs.fedoraproject.org** -- Elected technical steering committees with defined scope and transparent records provide sustainable peer governance for large distributions.


### Evidence That Complicates

**w3.org** -- Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes for peer participation.

**drupal.org** -- Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model requires years of structural work, not organic emergence.

### Synthesis

The cases suggest that sustainable heartbeat emerges from shared stakes, not shared schedules. The technical steering committees that work aren't the ones with the most meetings — they're the ones where members feel responsible for outcomes they can't achieve alone. Authority creates pulse. Responsibility creates rhythm.

What remains untested is whether you can design for this kind of organic heartbeat, or if it only emerges when the alternative is watching something you care about die. The useful friction may be in accepting that some collaborations don't deserve to survive their first missed meeting — and that this selection pressure is what keeps the real heartbeats strong.
