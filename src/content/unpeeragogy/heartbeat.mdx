### The Assumption

Regular meetings and check-ins provide rhythm and continuity for peer learning groups, keeping participants connected and projects moving forward through consistent touchpoints. Scheduled interaction creates sustainable collaboration.

### Operational Reality

A weekly standup call dies after six weeks. The Slack channel goes quiet. Three people still care about the project, but nobody knows who they are. This happens to every distributed team that mistakes communication for connection — they schedule heartbeats without checking if anyone has a pulse.

Not ritual. Not regular check-ins. Not another meeting where people report what they did yesterday. The heartbeat that actually works is the one you feel when something breaks. When the server goes down and two people jump on a call at midnight. When someone posts a half-formed idea and three others start building on it immediately. When the work itself generates the rhythm.

Most teams engineer their heartbeat backwards. They create the meeting first, then hope motivation follows. But motivation does not respond to calendar invites.

OpenStreetMap shows that heartbeat works when backed by authority, not scheduling. Fedora demonstrates that decision-making authority creates pulse more reliably than calendar invites. WebAssembly shows that heartbeat without authority creates rhythm without substance. Drupal shows that sustainable heartbeat requires governance design, not meeting scheduling.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if meetings without authority are just expensive group therapy sessions — and the strongest heartbeat is the one that skips a beat, the irregular pulse of people who only connect when they actually need each other?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [OpenStreetMap Data Working Group](https://wiki.osmfoundation.org/wiki/Data_Working_Group) — Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot. Heartbeat works when backed by authority, not scheduling.
- [Fedora Engineering Steering Committee (FESCo)](https://docs.fedoraproject.org/en-US/fesco/) — Elected technical steering committees with defined scope and transparent records provide sustainable peer governance. Decision-making authority creates pulse more reliably than calendar invites.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [WebAssembly Community Group](https://www.w3.org/community/webassembly/) — Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes. Heartbeat without authority creates rhythm without substance.
- [Drupal Governance](https://www.drupal.org/governance) — Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model required years of structural work. Sustainable heartbeat requires governance design, not meeting scheduling.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Apply this pattern when regular meetings are supported by formal dispute resolution mechanisms and defined decision-making authority — authority creates pulse; the calendar does not.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not apply this pattern when the group faces contested decisions or resource conflicts without formal authority structures — trading authority for speed creates meetings without substance.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The evidence suggests sustainable heartbeat emerges from shared stakes, not shared calendars. The technical committees that function are not the ones with more meetings — they are the ones where members feel responsible for outcomes they cannot achieve alone. Authority creates pulse. Responsibility creates rhythm. The useful friction might be accepting that some collaborations do not deserve to survive their first missed meeting.
