### Reality

The Peeragogy Handbook lists six antipatterns like items in a field guide to dysfunction. Stuck. Magical Thinking. Stasis. Each gets its own page, its own definition, its own neat boundaries. The assumption runs deeper than the categories — that naming the problem halfway solves it.

But Wikipedia's consensus processes expose the gap between theory and practice. The project formally operates on peer equality, yet participation inequality creates de facto hierarchies. Those who show up consistently gain disproportionate influence — not through declared authority but through sustained presence. The antipattern isn't in the handbook. It emerges from the very solution the handbook would prescribe.

Not documentation. Not governance boundaries. Not formal dispute resolution. The friction lives in the space between what we say we're doing and what actually happens when humans gather around shared work.

> What if antipatterns aren't problems to solve but mirrors we'd rather not look into?

### Evidence That Supports

**about.gitlab.com** -- Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.

**wiki.osmfoundation.org** -- Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot.

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that antipatterns may function less as diagnostic tools than as comfortable distance from uncomfortable truths. Wikipedia's informal hierarchies persist precisely because they serve functions that formal equality cannot — they create accountability, continuity, institutional memory. The dysfunction is also the system working.

Corporate open-core projects document governance boundaries not to eliminate friction but to make it visible and manageable. The antipattern becomes the pattern — useful friction that prevents worse outcomes. What remains to be tested is whether our discomfort with these emergent hierarchies reflects the problem or our own resistance to how peer production actually scales.
