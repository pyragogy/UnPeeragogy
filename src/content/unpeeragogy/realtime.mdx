### Claim

Real-time meetings create opportunities for immediate feedback and dynamic collaboration, allowing groups to adapt quickly and build on each other's contributions in the moment.

### Reality

Three programmers join a video call to debug a critical system failure. The first lives in Berlin, just finished dinner. The second connects from São Paulo during lunch break. The third dials in from San Francisco at 6 AM, coffee still brewing. They share screens, point at code, interrupt each other with sudden insights. The bug gets fixed in forty minutes.

Six months later, the same team tries to decide whether to rewrite their authentication system. The Berlin programmer has strong opinions about security architecture. São Paulo wants to ship fast. San Francisco stays quiet — it's 5 AM this time, and the decision feels too big for sleepy judgment. They schedule another call. Then another. The decision drifts for weeks because real-time works for debugging but breaks down when the stakes get higher and the timezone math gets uglier.

Not every problem fits the rhythm of a video call. Not every voice carries the same weight when some people are sharp and others are barely awake. Not every decision should be made by whoever shows up.

The human part gets lost when we mistake presence for participation.

> > What if the people who can't make the meeting are exactly the ones whose absence tells you the most about what you're really deciding?"*

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that real-time communication carries a hidden selection pressure — it favors those whose schedules, energy levels, and communication styles align with the dominant group's rhythm. The programmer who thinks best in writing gets drowned out by the one who thinks out loud. The parent who can't join evening calls gradually loses influence over decisions that shape their daily work.

What holds in small, urgent situations — the bug fix, the crisis response — may fracture when applied to larger questions that require reflection rather than reaction. The same immediacy that makes real-time powerful for coordination makes it dangerous for governance, where the quality of a decision often depends on who wasn't in the room to rush it through.
