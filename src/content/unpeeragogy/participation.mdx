### Reality

Three developers meet weekly for six months to build an open-source tool. By month four, only one still attends regularly. The project continues — decisions get made, code gets written, releases happen. The community adjusts to this reality: one person carries the work while two others drift in occasionally with opinions but no sustained commitment. This isn't dysfunction. It's what participation actually looks like when you strip away the mythology of equal engagement.

Not shared ownership. Not distributed leadership. Not collective decision-making where everyone has equal voice because everyone showed up equally. The person who debugged the authentication system at 2 AM on a Tuesday gets to decide how authentication works going forward.

The handbook promises equilibrium between order and chaos, everyone involved at their own pace. What it doesn't mention: pace determines power. Consistency trumps consensus when someone needs to ship code that actually runs.

> What if the real test of peer governance isn't how well it handles equal participation, but how honestly it acknowledges unequal commitment?

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that participation creates its own hierarchies through the simple mathematics of showing up. The developer who commits code weekly carries more weight than the one who appears monthly with feedback. The Wikipedia editor who monitors recent changes daily shapes policy more than occasional contributors who voice strong opinions in talk pages.

This may be less about formal versus informal governance and more about recognizing that sustained attention becomes informal authority. Lighter governance models don't eliminate power differentials — they make them less visible, harder to challenge, more dependent on endurance than expertise.

What remains untested: whether acknowledging these participation inequalities openly creates better peer dynamics than pretending they don't exist — or whether honesty about unequal commitment simply accelerates the drift toward informal hierarchy.
