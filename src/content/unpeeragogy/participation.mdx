### The Assumption

Most members in a peer group do not participate, only a few do. Participation is often seen as a cost rather than a benefit, and lack of recognition leads to decreased participation.

### Operational Reality

Three wolves and a sheep vote on dinner. Democracy in action. Peeragogy replaces the three wolves with the people who show up to every meeting, the sheep with the people who could not make the call because they had childcare. The vote is unanimous. Everyone consented. The people who did not vote now eat what the regulars decided.

Not manipulation. Not bad faith. Just the physics of participation inequality.

OpenStreetMap's Data Working Group exists precisely because informal consensus failed to resolve data disputes. The people who showed up consistently had disproportionate influence, and the people who did not show up lost trust in the process. Formal dispute resolution with binding authority did not reduce participation — it protected the legitimacy of decisions by making authority explicit.

Participation inequality is not a bug. It is a feature of any system where attendance is voluntary and stakes are unequal. The question is not how to fix it, but how to govern its consequences. WebAssembly shows that participation inequality persists regardless of governance weight — lighter models just make it harder to challenge. Wikipedia's deletion debates show that participation creates authority and transparent consensus legitimation reifies existing hierarchies.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if democracy is two wolves and a sheep voting on dinner — and peeragogy is three wolves who showed up to every meeting deciding what counts as consensus?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [OpenStreetMap Data Working Group](https://wiki.osmfoundation.org/wiki/Data_Working_Group) — Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot. Explicit authority structures protect legitimacy when participation is unequal.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [WebAssembly Community Group](https://www.w3.org/community/webassembly/) — Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes. Participation inequality persists regardless of governance weight — lighter models just make it harder to challenge.
- [Wikipedia Deletion Debates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Articles_for_deletion) — Consensus processes produce de facto hierarchies through participation inequality — those who show up consistently gain disproportionate influence. Participation creates authority and transparent consensus legitimation reifies existing hierarchies.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** This pattern works when governance structures are designed explicitly to manage participation inequality — participation creates authority; governance design either manages it explicitly or suffers it as default.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not assume informal consensus can handle all conflicts in groups with unequal participation — formal dispute resolution exists precisely because informal consensus failed to resolve disputes that required binding decisions with published reasoning.

### Epistemic Synthesis

Participation inequality is not a bug. It is a feature of any system where attendance is voluntary and stakes are unequal. OpenStreetMap created a Data Working Group not to increase participation but to protect the legitimacy of decisions in the face of structurally unequal participation. The tension is not between inclusion and efficiency, but between those who participate and those who hold authority.
