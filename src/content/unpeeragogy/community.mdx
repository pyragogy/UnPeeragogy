### Il Dogma

A community is "a space for learning together" where people "produce together" through shared engagement and interaction. The theory assumes that bringing people into proximity — whether physical or digital — creates the conditions for collaborative learning, and that community emerges naturally from shared intention.

### La Realtà

The Debian project has 1,000+ developers and runs on servers worldwide. But ask who decides package policy and you get a web of committees, technical leads, and informal influence networks. Not democracy. Not hierarchy. Something messier.

Most community-building advice assumes you're starting from zero — find your people, establish norms, scale gradually. But you're already embedded in multiple overlapping groups: your workplace, your neighborhood, the maintainers of software you depend on. The question isn't how to build community. It's how to navigate the communities that already claim pieces of your attention.

Corporate open-source projects document their governance boundaries precisely because the alternative — ambiguous authority between sponsor and contributors — kills momentum faster than any technical debt.

### Perturbatore

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if communities don't fail because people stop caring — they fail because nobody knows who gets to say no?"*

### Evidenze di Tenuta

**about.gitlab.com** — Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects. GitLab demonstrates that transparent corporate-community boundaries enable sustained collaboration.

### Evidenze di Faglia

**lists.debian.org** — Regular leadership summaries (DPL bits newsletter) in peer-governed projects create agenda-setting power. What gets summarized becomes what deserves attention. The summary-writers become the community's unconscious editors — a form of invisible authority the theory doesn't account for.

**w3.org** — Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes. WebAssembly shows that communities claiming peer production without clear governance boundaries suffer authority drift under pressure.

### Euristiche Operative

**Regola Positiva:** Applica questo pattern quando i confini di governance sono documentati esplicitamente e comunicati regolarmente. GitLab dimostra che confini trasparenti tra sponsor e comunità permettono collaborazione sostenuta.

**Regola Negativa:** Non applicare questo pattern quando le strutture di autorità sono implicite o indefinite. Il Peeragogy Project stesso mostra che comunità che reclamano peer production senza confini di governance chiari soffrono di authority drift — la decisione reale migra silenziosamente verso chi ha più tempo, non più competenza.

**Stato:** PROVVISORIO

La teoria assume che la comunità emerga naturalmente dall'interazione condivisa. L'evidenza mostra che le comunità funzionano quando qualcuno ha disegnato i confini — non tra insider e outsider, ma tra chi decide cosa. Le comunità non falliscono perché le persone smettono di preoccuparsi. Falliscono perché nessuno sa chi può dire no.
