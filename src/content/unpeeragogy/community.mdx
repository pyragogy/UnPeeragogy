### The Assumption

A community is "a space for learning together" where people "produce together" through shared engagement and interaction. Bringing people into proximity — whether physical or digital — creates the conditions for collaborative learning, and community emerges naturally from shared intention.

### Operational Reality

The Debian project has 1,000+ developers and runs on servers worldwide. Ask who decides package policy and you get a web of committees, technical leads, and informal influence networks. Not democracy. Not hierarchy. Something messier.

Most community-building advice assumes you are starting from zero — find your people, establish norms, scale gradually. But you are already embedded in multiple overlapping groups: your workplace, your neighborhood, the maintainers of software you depend on. The question is not how to build community. It is how to navigate the communities that already claim pieces of your attention.

Corporate open-source projects that document governance boundaries — precisely because the alternative, ambiguous authority between sponsor and contributors — kill momentum faster than any technical debt. GitLab's handbook shows that explicit documentation of governance boundaries reduces friction. Debian's DPL bits newsletter shows that regular leadership summaries in peer-governed projects create agenda-setting power — what gets summarized becomes what deserves attention. WebAssembly demonstrates that communities claiming peer production without clear governance boundaries suffer authority drift under pressure.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if communities don't fail because people stop caring — they fail because nobody knows who gets to say no?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [GitLab Stewardship Handbook](https://about.gitlab.com/handbook/company/stewardship/) — Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects. Transparent corporate-community boundaries enable sustained collaboration.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [Debian DPL Bits Newsletter](https://lists.debian.org/debian-devel-announce/) — Regular leadership summaries create agenda-setting power in peer-governed projects. What gets summarized becomes what deserves attention. The summary-writers become the community's unconscious editors — a form of invisible authority the theory does not account for.
- [WebAssembly Community Group](https://www.w3.org/community/webassembly/) — Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes. Communities claiming peer production without clear governance boundaries suffer authority drift under pressure.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Apply community patterns when governance boundaries are documented explicitly and communicated regularly — transparent boundaries between sponsor and community enable sustained collaboration.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not assume community emerges naturally from shared intention when authority structures are implicit or undefined — communities that claim peer production without clear governance boundaries suffer authority drift; real decision-making migrates silently to those with more time, not more competence.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The theory assumes community emerges naturally from shared interaction. Evidence shows that communities function when someone has designed the boundaries — not between insider and outsider, but between who decides what. Communities do not fail because people stop caring. They fail because nobody knows who can say no.
