### Reality

The Debian project has 1,000+ developers and runs on servers worldwide. But ask who decides package policy and you get a web of committees, technical leads, and informal influence networks. Not democracy. Not hierarchy. Something messier.

Most community-building advice assumes you're starting from zero — find your people, establish norms, scale gradually. But you're already embedded in multiple overlapping groups: your workplace, your neighborhood, the maintainers of software you depend on. The question isn't how to build community. It's how to navigate the communities that already claim pieces of your attention.

Corporate open-source projects document their governance boundaries precisely because the alternative — ambiguous authority between sponsor and contributors — kills momentum faster than any technical debt.

> What if the communities we can't see are the ones that actually shape what we can do?

### Evidence That Complicates

### Evidence That Is Ambiguous

**w3.org** -- Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes for peer participation.


### Synthesis

Regular leadership summaries in peer-governed projects create a subtle agenda-setting power. What gets summarized becomes what deserves attention. What doesn't get mentioned slowly disappears from collective memory. The summary-writers become the community's unconscious editors.

This suggests that community formation might be less about bringing people together and more about making visible the decision-making patterns that already exist. The useful friction comes from exposing who actually gets to say no — not in theory, but when resources are scarce and choices must be made.

What remains to be tested: whether communities can survive their own transparency about power.
