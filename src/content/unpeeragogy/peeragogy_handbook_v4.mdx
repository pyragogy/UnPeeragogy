### Reality

Someone wrote this handbook. Not you. Not me. Someone else decided what counted as peeragogy, what belonged in version 4, what got translated into which format. The colophon lists contact emails and license terms — the bureaucracy of knowledge production — but no names attached to the actual thinking.

This matters because handbooks aren't neutral. They're arguments disguised as reference materials. Every section included means ten others got cut. Every framework presented means alternative approaches got buried. The Peeragogy Handbook positions itself as community knowledge, but communities don't write handbooks — individuals do, then communities inherit the constraints.

Not organic emergence. Not collective intelligence. Not the wisdom of crowds. Someone sat down and decided what peeragogy means, then wrapped that decision in the language of peer collaboration. The handbook exists because someone claimed authority to define the field, then handed you the result as if it grew naturally from practice.

> What if the most peer-to-peer act would be to write your own handbook from scratch?

### Synthesis

The evidence suggests something uncomfortable: governance boundaries work when they're explicit, but most peer learning projects inherit implicit hierarchies they never examine. The handbook's anonymous authorship isn't transparency — it's a way of avoiding responsibility for the choices embedded in every chapter structure and theoretical framework.

What remains untested is whether true peer learning can survive its own documentation. Handbooks freeze practice into theory. They turn living experiments into dead knowledge. The tension between needing shared references and maintaining the useful friction of genuine collaboration — this doesn't resolve cleanly into better governance models or clearer attribution practices.

The handbook was written by someone else, which means you're always reading their version of what peer learning could be, not discovering what it might become.
