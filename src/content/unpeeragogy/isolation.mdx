### Claim

Many projects that are ostensibly oriented towards "the commons"

### Reality

A founder builds their project for three years. Contributors come and go, but the core decisions — architecture, roadmap, culture — flow through one person. The project grows. New contributors want input on direction. The founder announces a transition to community governance, sets up committees, writes bylaws. Six months later, the project fragments into competing forks.

Not because the founder was authoritarian. Not because the community was ungrateful. Not because governance models don't work.

Because power doesn't redistribute itself through good intentions.

The invisible part happens in the months before the announcement — who gets consulted on the transition plan, whose concerns shape the new structure, which voices the founder has learned to trust. By the time governance becomes "open," the real decisions have already been made by people who earned influence through proximity, not contribution.

> What if isolation isn't about excluding others, but about who gets to define inclusion?

### Synthesis

The governance transition cases suggest something uncomfortable about peer collaboration. The problem isn't narrow focus versus wide chaos — it's that someone always defines the spectrum. Even in the most open projects, participation gets shaped by invisible architectures of influence.

The founder who transitions to community governance may genuinely want to share power. But the transition itself becomes a demonstration of retained authority — the power to decide how power gets shared. The community that emerges reflects not collective wisdom but the founder's theory of what collective wisdom should look like.

What remains to be tested is whether genuine peer governance can emerge from anything other than crisis — whether distributed authority requires the collapse of centralized control, not its graceful handover.
