### Reality

The Peeragogy Handbook offers a reading list. Reagle on Wikipedia's good faith. Kano on judo's educational value. Wallace's unfinished novel about boredom. Each citation carries the weight of academic legitimacy — proof that peer learning has intellectual ancestors, theoretical foundations, proper footnotes.

I've watched dozens of learning communities die under the weight of their own reading lists. Not from bad books. From the assumption that shared knowledge begins with shared texts. The anarchist collective that spent three months arguing over whether to read Kropotkin or Bookchin before doing any actual organizing. The maker space that required new members to complete a "foundational reading" before touching tools. The study group that became a performance of having read rather than a place where learning happened.

Books don't create peers. They create hierarchies of who has read what.

> What if the most radical act in peer learning is refusing to assign anything at all?

### Synthesis

The evidence suggests something more complex than anti-intellectualism. Explicit governance documentation does reduce friction — when it emerges from lived conflict, not prescribed theory. Peer assessment works — when criteria develop through practice, not imported frameworks. The transition from single authority to distributed leadership requires structural work — but that work happens through doing, not through studying how others have done it.

What remains untested is whether communities can hold the tension between learning and reading — using texts as tools rather than foundations, reaching for books when the work demands them rather than before the work begins.
