### Reality

George Brett took selfies with a rubber duckie. This detail appears in the preface to a handbook on peer learning — not buried in footnotes, but front-loaded as dedication. The editors chose to open their theory with yellow rubber and mail art, then pivot to "expanding the educational." The sentence breaks mid-thought.

Most prefaces perform institutional theater. They thank the right people, cite the proper authorities, establish credibility through association. Not this one. Not rubber duckie credibility. Not mail art as educational expansion.

The preface refuses its own genre.

> What if the most honest thing you can say about peer learning is that it starts with someone who brought a rubber duckie to the conversation?

### Synthesis

The evidence suggests something the theory won't admit — peer learning needs institutional scaffolding to survive contact with reality. Governance boundaries, defined facilitator roles, partnerships. The infrastructure that keeps the duckie afloat.

But George Brett's presence in the preface exposes the gap between peer learning as practiced and peer learning as performed. The human part that resists systematization. What remains to be tested is whether honesty about this friction strengthens the work — or whether some contradictions are too useful to resolve.
