### Reality

Mrs. Chen locks her classroom door at 3:15 PM and drives home with a stack of papers to grade. She's been teaching fourth grade for twelve years. The district mandated a new reading curriculum this fall — the third change in five years. She attended the required training, nodded through the PowerPoint about "collaborative learning environments," then closed her door and taught the way that works for her kids. Not because she's stubborn. Because she's earned the right to know what holds.

The peeragogy handbook celebrates teachers who "generously share resources with one another." True enough. Teachers swap worksheets, trade classroom management tips, pass along which substitute actually shows up. But this isn't peeragogy — it's survival. The sharing happens despite the system, not because of it. When your evaluation depends on test scores and your principal changes every two years, you don't experiment with peer learning circles. You protect what works.

The friction isn't accidental.

> What if the isolation isn't the bug but the feature — the only space left where teaching can happen without measurement?

### Synthesis

These cases suggest something uncomfortable about institutional learning. The teacher who shares resources generously may also refuse to open her practice to peer observation. Not contradiction — strategy. She's learned to distinguish between what can be shared (a worksheet) and what must be protected (the rhythm of actual teaching). The handbook's vision of collaborative educators assumes a context that doesn't exist: time, trust, and freedom from external judgment.

What remains to be tested is whether peeragogy can survive contact with institutional accountability — or whether it requires the very isolation it claims to cure.
