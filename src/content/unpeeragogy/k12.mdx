### The Assumption

Peeragogy in K12 settings is nearly impossible due to compulsory attendance and fixed curriculum, with teachers maintaining control that hinders peer learning. Institutional authority is fundamentally incompatible with peer-to-peer learning dynamics, and peer methods can transform the classroom if given space.

### Operational Reality

The teacher locks her classroom door at 3:15 PM and drives home with a stack of papers to grade. Twelve years in. The district mandated a new reading curriculum this fall — the third change in five years. She attended the required training, nodded through the PowerPoint about "collaborative learning environments," then closed her door and taught the way that works for her kids. Not because she is stubborn. Because she has earned the right to know what holds.

The Peeragogy Handbook celebrates teachers who "generously share resources with one another." True enough. Teachers swap worksheets, trade classroom management tips, pass along which substitute actually shows up. But this is not peeragogy — it is survival. The sharing happens despite the system, not because of it. When your evaluation depends on test scores and your principal changes every two years, you do not experiment with peer learning circles. You protect what works. The friction is not accidental.

GitLab shows that clear boundaries between authority and community can create protected peer spaces even within hierarchical structures. Drupal shows that moving from institutional authority to peer governance requires structural redesign, not just permission. arXiv demonstrates that even the most peer-driven systems need validation mechanisms that look like authority.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if you cannot peer-learn your way out of a report card — and the isolation is not the bug but the feature, the only space left where teaching can happen without measurement?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [GitLab Stewardship Handbook](https://about.gitlab.com/handbook/company/stewardship/) — Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects. Clear boundaries between authority and community can create protected peer spaces even within hierarchical structures.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [Drupal Governance](https://www.drupal.org/governance) — Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model required years of structural work, not organic emergence. Moving from institutional authority to peer governance requires structural redesign, not just permission.
- [arXiv Endorsement System](https://arxiv.org/help/endorsement) — Peer assessment systems in scholarly communication require endorsement hierarchies that contradict the egalitarian ideal. Even the most peer-driven systems need validation mechanisms that look like authority.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Apply this pattern when governance boundaries are documented explicitly and peer spaces are carved inside — not against — institutional frameworks. Clear boundaries between authority and community reduce friction.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not attempt peeragogy in K12 contexts without explicitly negotiating boundaries with institutional authority. The assumption that authority and peer learning are incompatible leads to unrealistic attempts that consume energy without producing sustainable change.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The teacher who shares resources but refuses peer observation is not contradicting peer learning — she is distinguishing strategically between what can be shared (a worksheet) and what must be protected (the rhythm of actual teaching). The handbook's vision of collaborative educators assumes a context that does not exist: time, trust, and freedom from external judgment. The friction is not accidental — it is the rational response to a system where individual accountability and collaborative innovation are in structural competition.
