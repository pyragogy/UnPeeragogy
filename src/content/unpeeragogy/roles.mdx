### Claim

Educational interactions tend to have a number of different roles

### Reality

The technical steering committee meets every two weeks on Zoom. Seven volunteers review pull requests, debate architectural decisions, make binding choices about the project's future. Their names appear in the GOVERNANCE.md file. Their authority stems from community election, their scope from written charter, their accountability from public meeting notes. This looks like peer governance working.

The same three people do most of the talking. Two others attend but rarely speak. The remaining two show up sporadically, citing burnout from their day jobs. When a contentious decision arrives — say, breaking API compatibility — the vocal minority drives the outcome. The quiet majority either agrees by silence or stops attending entirely. Not hierarchy in the traditional sense. Not peer equality either. Something else — the drift toward informal power concentration that no charter can prevent.

The DAO promises different mechanics. Token holders vote on proposals. Smart contracts execute decisions automatically. No human gatekeepers, no informal influence networks. Yet the largest token holder owns 12% of the supply, the top ten hold 47%. Their votes carry more weight than thousands of smaller participants combined. The system runs on mathematical equality — one token, one vote — while producing aristocratic outcomes.

> What if the problem isn't bad roles but the pretense that roles don't exist?

### Evidence That Supports

**docs.fedoraproject.org** -- Elected technical steering committees with defined scope and transparent records provide sustainable peer governance for large distributions.


### Evidence That Complicates

**lwn.net** -- Peer governance models that concentrate review authority on volunteers create burnout failure points that hierarchy would mitigate through role redundancy.

**lido.fi** -- Token-based governance in DAOs concentrates power among large holders, creating de facto plutocracy rather than peer equality.

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that peer structures don't eliminate hierarchy — they obscure it. The steering committee creates formal equality while informal influence patterns emerge through participation gaps and communication styles. The DAO distributes power mathematically while economic inequality recreates the same concentration through different mechanics.

What remains untested is whether acknowledging these power differentials openly produces better outcomes than maintaining the fiction of peer equality. The useful friction may lie not in designing perfect peer systems, but in making visible the hierarchies that form anyway — naming who actually decides, who gets heard, who burns out maintaining the infrastructure that others take for granted.

The distinction worth holding: between systems that admit their power structures and systems that hide them behind peer rhetoric.
