### The Assumption

Roles in peer learning should be fluid and rotational to prevent hierarchy formation and maintain equality among participants.

### Operational Reality

Rotating the deck chairs does not sink the Titanic any slower.

The pattern assumes fluid roles prevent hierarchy. They do not. They just make hierarchy invisible — harder to see, harder to challenge, harder to name. Fedora's Engineering Steering Committee shows that elected roles with defined scope and public records maintain peer governance precisely because the hierarchy is explicit and bounded. The Linux kernel's maintainer burnout shows that unlimited peer responsibility creates failure points that rotation alone cannot address. Lido DAO demonstrates that even blockchain-based peer governance centralizes when fluid roles meet real decisions.

Fluid roles feel egalitarian. They are also unaccountable. The person who chaired last month's meeting is not responsible for last month's decision.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if rotating the deck chairs doesn't sink the Titanic any slower — and fluid roles feel egalitarian but are also unaccountable?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [Fedora Engineering Steering Committee](https://docs.fedoraproject.org/en-US/fesco/) — Elected technical steering committees with defined scope and transparent records provide sustainable peer governance. Transparent hierarchy enables peer production.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [Linux Kernel Maintainer Burnout](https://lwn.net/Articles/885941/) — Unlimited peer responsibility creates failure points that rotation alone cannot address. Fluid responsibility without bounded authority produces burnout.
- [Lido DAO Governance](https://lido.fi/governance) — Even blockchain-based peer governance centralizes when fluid roles meet real decisions. Explicit hierarchy is more accountable than invisible hierarchy.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Roles work when authority scope is explicitly bounded and transparent — elected technical roles with defined scope and public records maintain peer production.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not apply fluid roles when decisions require specialized knowledge or carry long-term maintenance burden — unlimited peer responsibility creates failure points; maintainer burnout is a governance risk, not an individual one.

### Epistemic Synthesis

Fluid roles do not prevent hierarchy — they make it invisible. Fedora shows that explicit, bounded hierarchy enables peer production. Linux shows that unlimited responsibility produces burnout regardless of rotation. Lido shows that even blockchain governance centralizes when fluid roles meet real decisions. The chair of last month's meeting is not responsible for last month's decision. That is the cost of fluidity.
