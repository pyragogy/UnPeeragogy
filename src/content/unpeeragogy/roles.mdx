## Summary

Roles emerge to hide power, not distribute it. Theory says peer governance flattens hierarchy — practice shows roles just make authority invisible. The question isn't whether you have roles, but whether you can see them working.

## Euristiche Operative

### Positive Rule

> This pattern works when authority scope is explicitly bounded and transparent. Fedora Engineering Steering Committee shows that elected technical roles with defined scope and public records maintain peer governance while preventing both burnout and shadow hierarchies.

### Negative Rule

> Do not apply fluid roles when decisions require specialized knowledge or have long-term maintenance burden. Linux kernel maintainer burnout shows that unlimited peer responsibility creates failure points that structured roles with redundancy could prevent.

## Sintesi Obliqua

The pattern claims fluid roles prevent hierarchy while maintaining peer governance. Partial verification — when scope is bounded and records are transparent, elected technical committees like Fedora's FESCo do sustain peer decision-making without shadow authority. But Linux kernel maintainer burnout and Lido DAO token concentration show that unbounded peer responsibility creates failure points that structured redundancy could prevent. The mechanism missing here is how roles become visible before they become problems. Authority doesn't disappear when you refuse to name it.
