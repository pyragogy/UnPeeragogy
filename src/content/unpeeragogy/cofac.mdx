### Il Dogma

Co-facilitation distributes authority and prevents single points of failure in peer learning groups. When multiple people share facilitation duties, the group becomes more resilient and democratic. The theory assumes that shared facilitation naturally produces better outcomes through distributed responsibility.

### La Realtà

Two facilitators sit across from each other in a video call that started forty minutes late. The group they're supposed to guide dissolved three weeks ago — half the members stopped showing up, the other half argue in private channels about who's actually in charge. This isn't peer learning failing. This is co-facilitation eating itself.

Not shared leadership. Not distributed authority. Not collective wisdom emerging from the group mind. Co-facilitation becomes a performance of equality that nobody can afford to maintain. The work doubles while the clarity halves — two people checking with each other before every decision, two people responsible when things fall apart, two people who can't move fast enough to catch problems before they metastasize.

Watch any peer learning experiment that lasted more than six months. The ones that survived didn't distribute facilitation — they concentrated it, then built systems around that concentration that looked like distribution.

### Perturbatore

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if the burden of co-facilitation isn't the extra coordination, but pretending authority doesn't exist when someone always ends up holding it anyway?"*

### Evidenze di Tenuta

**p2pu.org** — Peer learning models with defined facilitator roles and institutional partnerships are more sustainable than purely peer-driven learning. P2PU shows that role clarity — not distributed ambiguity — enables resilience.

**wiki.osmfoundation.org** — Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot. OpenStreetMap demonstrates that some conflicts require singular authority, not shared facilitation.

### Evidenze di Faglia

**drupal.org** — Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model required years of structural work, not organic emergence. Drupal shows that distributing authority is a multi-year structural project, not a facilitation choice.

**ietf.org** — Rough consensus governance only works when backed by hierarchical escalation paths. The IETF shows that even the most peer-driven bodies need singular appeals paths — co-facilitation without backup is organized procrastination with extra meetings.

### Euristiche Operative

**Regola Positiva:** Applica la co-facilitazione solo quando supportata da meccanismi formali di risoluzione dispute e continuità istituzionale. P2PU e IETF dimostrano che partnership istituzionali e percorsi di escalation gerarchici rendono la facilitazione condivisa sostenibile.

**Regola Negativa:** Non applicare la co-facilitazione quando al gruppo mancano percorsi di escalation formali o supporto istituzionale. IETF e OpenStreetMap dimostrano che il consenso senza appelli crea conflitti irrisolvibili — e due facilitatori senza autorità sono peggio di uno con autorità.

### Sintesi & Tracciabilità

**Riferimento:** audit-v2 (delta tensione: 1.3 → 1.3319, +0.0319)
**Evidenze esaminate:** 4 (2 confermanti, 2 complicanti)
**Stato:** PROVVISORIO

L'assunzione teorica — che distribuire la facilitazione aumenti resilienza e democrazia — è sistematicamente contraddetta. Ogni caso di successo nell'archivio concentrava l'autorità, poi costruiva sistemi intorno a quella concentrazione che sembravano distribuzione. La co-facilitazione senza piani di backup è procrastinazione organizzata con riunioni extra.
