## Summary

Co-facilitation multiplies coordination overhead without solving the authority problem. Theory promises shared responsibility — practice delivers shared blame when decisions stall. The real cost isn't the extra meetings, it's the illusion that consensus scales.

## Euristiche Operative

### Positive Rule

> Co-facilitation works when backed by formal dispute resolution mechanisms and institutional continuity. P2PU learning circles with institutional partnerships and IETF working groups with hierarchical appeals show that hybrid peer-institutional models sustain cooperation across years and conflicts.

### Negative Rule

> Do not apply co-facilitation when groups lack formal escalation paths or institutional support. The IETF and OpenStreetMap cases show that consensus without appeals creates unresolvable conflicts, while P2PU demonstrates that purely peer-driven models collapse without institutional backing.

## Sintesi Obliqua

The pattern claims distributed facilitation reduces single points of failure and democratizes leadership. Partial verification — but only when formal escalation paths exist. P2PU learning circles work because institutional partnerships provide backup authority, OpenStreetMap's Data Working Group resolves conflicts because it has binding power. Drupal's community working groups and purely peer-driven models collapse because consensus without appeals creates permanent deadlock. The mechanism isn't co-facilitation itself but the shadow of formal authority that makes informal cooperation possible. Two facilitators arguing is still just an argument.
