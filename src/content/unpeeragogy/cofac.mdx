### Reality

Two facilitators sit across from each other in a video call that started forty minutes late. The group they're supposed to guide dissolved three weeks ago — half the members stopped showing up, the other half argue in private channels about who's actually in charge. This isn't peer learning failing. This is co-facilitation eating itself.

Not shared leadership. Not distributed authority. Not collective wisdom emerging from the group mind. Co-facilitation becomes a performance of equality that nobody can afford to maintain. The work doubles while the clarity halves — two people checking with each other before every decision, two people responsible when things fall apart, two people who can't move fast enough to catch problems before they metastasize.

Watch any peer learning experiment that lasted more than six months. The ones that survived didn't distribute facilitation — they concentrated it, then built systems around that concentration that looked like distribution.

> What if the burden of co-facilitation isn't in the extra coordination, but in pretending that authority doesn't exist when someone always ends up holding it anyway?

### Evidence That Supports

**p2pu.org** -- Peer learning models with defined facilitator roles and institutional partnerships are more sustainable than purely peer-driven learning.

**wiki.osmfoundation.org** -- Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot.


### Evidence That Complicates

**drupal.org** -- Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model requires years of structural work, not organic emergence.

**ietf.org** -- Rough consensus governance only works when backed by hierarchical escalation paths; pure consensus without appeals fails.

### Synthesis

The evidence suggests something uncomfortable about peer learning's relationship with power. Groups that acknowledge hierarchy — even temporary, rotating hierarchy — outlast groups that perform consensus while practicing informal dominance. The community governance transitions that work take years of structural preparation, not because the community needs time to learn collaboration, but because real authority transfer requires legal frameworks, conflict resolution systems, and explicit succession planning.

This may explain why formal dispute resolution succeeds where consensus fails. Not because peers can't govern themselves, but because governance without appeals courts becomes governance by whoever can outlast the argument. What remains to be tested — whether co-facilitation serves the group or serves the facilitators' need to avoid being seen as leaders.
