### The Assumption

Co-facilitation distributes authority and prevents single points of failure in peer learning groups. When multiple people share facilitation duties, the group becomes more resilient and democratic. Shared facilitation naturally produces better outcomes through distributed responsibility.

### Operational Reality

Two facilitators sit across from each other in a meeting that started late. The group they are supposed to guide dissolved weeks ago — half the members stopped showing up, the other half argue in private channels about who is actually in charge. This is not peer learning failing. This is co-facilitation eating itself.

Not shared leadership. Not distributed authority. Not collective wisdom. Co-facilitation becomes a performance of equality that nobody can afford to maintain. The work doubles while the clarity halves — two people checking with each other before every decision, two people responsible when things fall apart, two people who cannot move fast enough to catch problems before they metastasize.

The peer learning experiments that lasted more than six months did not distribute facilitation — they concentrated it, then built systems around that concentration that looked like distribution.

P2PU shows that role clarity — not distributed ambiguity — enables resilience. OpenStreetMap shows that some conflicts require singular authority, not shared facilitation. Drupal shows that distributing authority is a multi-year structural project, not a facilitation choice. The IETF shows that even the most peer-driven bodies need singular appeals paths — co-facilitation without backup is organized procrastination with extra meetings.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if the burden of co-facilitation is not the extra coordination, but pretending authority does not exist when someone always ends up holding it anyway?"*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [P2PU Learning Circles](https://www.p2pu.org/en/learning-circles/) — Peer learning models with defined facilitator roles and institutional partnerships are more sustainable than purely peer-driven learning. Role clarity — not distributed ambiguity — enables resilience.
- [OpenStreetMap Data Working Group](https://wiki.osmfoundation.org/wiki/Data_Working_Group) — Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority resolve conflicts that informal consensus cannot. Some conflicts require singular authority, not shared facilitation.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [Drupal Governance](https://www.drupal.org/governance) — Governance transition from BDFL to community model required years of structural work. Distributing authority is a multi-year structural project, not a facilitation choice.
- [IETF About](https://www.ietf.org/about/) — Rough consensus governance only works when backed by hierarchical escalation paths. Even the most peer-driven bodies need singular appeals paths — co-facilitation without backup is organized procrastination with extra meetings.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Apply co-facilitation only when supported by formal dispute resolution mechanisms and institutional continuity — institutional partnerships and hierarchical escalation paths make shared facilitation sustainable.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not apply co-facilitation when the group lacks formal escalation paths or institutional support — consensus without appeals creates irresolvable conflicts, and two facilitators without authority are worse than one with authority.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The theoretical assumption — that distributing facilitation increases resilience and democracy — is systematically contradicted. Every successful case in the archive concentrated authority, then built systems around that concentration that resembled distribution. Co-facilitation without backup is organized procrastination with extra meetings.
