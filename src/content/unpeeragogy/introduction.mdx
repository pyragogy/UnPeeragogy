### Reality

The Peeragogy Handbook opens with a confession of imperfection, then immediately breaks its own promise. "We're not going to pretend this book is perfect" — but the next 300 pages read like expert instruction manual, complete with patterns, principles, and prescribed methods. Howard Rheingold writes the foreword. Academic contributors dominate the contributor list. The structure follows textbook convention: theory first, examples second, reflection last.

Not peer learning. Not collaborative discovery. Not the messy, uncertain work of figuring things out together.

The governance evidence tells a different story — one the handbook doesn't address. When Mozilla restructured in 2020, community governance councils found their authority suddenly circumscribed by corporate decisions made in closed rooms. The elected representatives had transparent records and defined processes, but no power when it mattered. Peer governance works until it meets institutional reality.

The introduction promises adventure when "you get out your pen" — but adventures don't come with handbooks.

> What if introductions had to earn their promises through visible struggle rather than confident declarations?

### Evidence That Supports

**about.gitlab.com** -- Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.

**community.kde.org** -- Elected peer governance councils with defined authority and transparent records provide stable long-term community governance.


### Evidence That Complicates

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that peer learning may require different starting gestures than traditional education — not because the content differs, but because the relationship to authority must be established differently from the first encounter. The handbook's confession of imperfection reads as humility, but functions as expertise: "we know enough to know what we don't know." This may preserve exactly the teacher-student dynamic that peer learning claims to dissolve.

The governance evidence complicates this further. Even explicitly peer-designed structures remain vulnerable to forces they cannot govern — corporate restructuring, institutional priorities, resource allocation decisions made elsewhere. What holds in peer governance may be less the formal structures than the ongoing work of maintaining peer relationships across power differentials that never fully disappear.

The tension between promise and delivery in introductions — whether to handbooks or communities — may be where the real learning begins.
