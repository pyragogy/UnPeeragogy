### Reality

The governance handbook for Apache projects runs 47 pages. It defines roles, voting procedures, conflict resolution paths. Most Apache projects ignore 80% of it. They use IRC channels and whoever shows up decides. This works until it doesn't — then they discover they needed those 47 pages after all.

Not bureaucracy. Not process for its own sake. Not even formal authority.

The useful friction lives in the boundaries between what's documented and what actually happens. Kubernetes moved from Google's BDFL model to a community steering committee over three years. They didn't copy another project's constitution — they pressure-tested each governance layer against their specific contributor culture, their particular technical decisions, their earned trust patterns.

The human part resists templates.

> What if the consultant's "it depends" isn't evasion but the only honest answer to governance design?

### Evidence That Supports

**about.gitlab.com** -- Explicit documentation of governance boundaries between corporate sponsor and community reduces friction in open-core projects.

**wiki.osmfoundation.org** -- Formal dispute resolution bodies with binding authority and published reasoning resolve peer production conflicts that informal consensus cannot.


### Evidence That Complicates

**w3.org** -- Choosing lighter governance models for standards development trades authority for speed, creating different failure modes for peer participation.

**drupal.org** -- Intentional governance transition from BDFL to community model requires years of structural work, not organic emergence.

### Synthesis

These cases suggest that governance specificity creates its own constraints. The Apache model works for Apache because it emerged from their particular history of corporate-academic tension. Kubernetes governance reflects their container orchestration complexity and Google's release patterns. Neither transfers cleanly.

What may hold across contexts — the rhythm of documenting boundaries only after informal practice breaks down. The work of earning authority before claiming it. The struggle to resist both premature formalization and permanent informality.

What remains to be tested — whether peer production governance can acknowledge its own non-generalizability without abandoning the search for useful patterns.
