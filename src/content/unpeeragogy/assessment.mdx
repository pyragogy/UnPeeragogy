### The Assumption

Peers can evaluate each other honestly when the assessment process is transparent, collaborative, and the goal is shared growth. Self-assessment and peer feedback function as formative practices embedded in peer learning, not as gatekeeping mechanisms. The theory assumes that transparent criteria and shared purpose produce honest evaluation.

### Operational Reality

Peer assessment creates quality hierarchies. And those hierarchies conflict with the peer governance that produced them.

The problem is structural, not personal. Evaluation requires someone to say no — to reject a contribution, to name what is not good enough. In a peer group, saying no risks the relationship. So the group avoids it. Feedback becomes "nice work!" and the group never names what went wrong.

Wikipedia's Good Article process works for reasons the theory does not fully account for: not because peers evaluate each other, but because nominated reviewers with binding authority apply transparent criteria. It is not peer assessment. It is lightweight hierarchy operating under peer language.

Moodle's plugin QA system works the same way: nominated integrators review community contributions against clear criteria, explicitly authorized to say no. The community accepts the hierarchy because the criteria are transparent and the integrators are known.

The Wikipedia Featured Article Candidate process tests the same community with stricter criteria. The peer assessment system produces a contradiction: quality tiers create an elite, and the elite conflicts with the egalitarian ideals that the community claims. The system works technically. It struggles socially.

> **Perturbatore:** *"What if the problem is not the criteria but the courage to apply them? You can have the most transparent process in the world — if nobody is willing to reject a friend's contribution, the process produces nothing but paper."*

### Empirical Evidence

**Resilience Cases (Confirming):**
- [Wikipedia Good Article process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Good_articles) — Transparent criteria plus independent reviewers with binding authority create sustainable quality tiers. Reviewers can reject a nomination without the author's consent.
- [Moodle plugin QA system](https://moodle.org/plugins/) — Nominated integrators review community contributions against clear criteria. Scales because maintainers are explicitly authorized to say no.

**Fracture Cases (Complicating):**
- [Wikipedia Featured Article Candidate process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Featured_article_candidates) — Same community, stricter criteria. Quality tiers create an elite that conflicts with egalitarian community ideals. The system works technically but struggles socially.
- [Linux kernel Reviewed-by tag system](https://docs.kernel.org/process/submitting-patches.html) — Formal peer review creates accountability but concentrates the bottleneck: the limiting factor shifts from writing code to getting it reviewed. Peer assessment does not distribute the work evenly.

### Operational Heuristics

**Positive Boundary:** Apply peer assessment only when reviewers are explicitly authorized to reject and criteria are transparent — quality control requires someone with the authority to say no, and that authority must be structurally supported, not relationally negotiated.

**Negative Boundary:** Do not rely on peer assessment without a binding rejection mechanism — in the absence of authorized reviewers, the social cost of rejection suppresses honest evaluation and the group never learns what is not good enough.

### Epistemic Synthesis

The cases suggest a structural tension the theory does not resolve: assessment requires the authority to reject, but peer governance resists hierarchy. The evidence points to a practical compromise — transparent criteria, named reviewers, binding decisions — that works but does not resolve the underlying contradiction. Whether peer assessment without hierarchy is possible at all remains an open question. The evidence leans toward the interpretation that the phrase contains a contradiction that no amount of process design can fully resolve.
